\title[Stated or Sampled?]{Stated or Sampled? Post-Training Narrows a Language Model's Sampled Differential Diagnosis, Not the One It States}

\begin{abstract}
A language model's differential diagnosis can be read from the list it states in one answer or from the spread of its answers over repeated samples, the readout behind pass@$k$, self-consistency and sampling-based uncertainty. We compare the two readouts across base and post-trained versions of open 7--32B models from three families (Qwen, OLMo and Mistral) on DDXPlus, whose synthetic patients carry a reference differential, and on 897 published case reports.
Post-training narrows the sampled differential in every family: post-trained models give 1.3--2.4 distinct answers per 32 samples, against 3.8--4.9, and with each model prompted at its best they lose 13--30 points of pass@32 (7--18 after subtracting what their samples reach against another patient's diagnosis).
The stated list does not narrow. It stays close to the base model's averaged list for the same patient and varies little between calls, and for the smaller Qwen models it contains the true diagnosis more often.
Severe conditions, also from published must-not-miss lists, are named more often on average, but specific ones are lost.
Most of the first-answer gain of post-training disappears once base and post-trained models get the same prompt and decode greedily.
Majority voting stops helping and agreement between samples weakens as an error signal, so sampling-based tools understate what one post-trained answer contains; thinking mode restores agreement.
%MEDCASEABSTRACT
Studies of diagnostic breadth should report which readout they use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{pg:mainstart}
Clinicians reason over a differential diagnosis, a ranked set of conditions consistent with the presentation, and the conditions they keep in view determine which tests are ordered and which dangerous possibilities are ruled out.
A language model's differential can be read in two ways: from the list it states in one answer, or from the distribution of its answers over repeated samples.
Much current practice uses the second. Pass@$k$, self-consistency voting~\citep{wang2023selfconsistency} and sampling-based uncertainty~\citep{kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024semanticentropy} draw on repeated samples, and so does the evidence that reinforcement learning sharpens models, which win at $\passk{1}$ while their base models solve more problems given many samples~\citep{yue2025rlboundary,wu2025invisibleleash,oh2026sharpeningtax}.
The two readouts agree for a model that samples from the distribution it states. Instruction-tuned models do not: they verbalize distributions they fail to sample from~\citep{meister2025distributional,zhang2025verbalized,jang2026cannotsample}, and one aligned answer can cover what many base-model samples spread out~\citep{lake2025overton}. We ask what this means for differential diagnosis, where every case has a reference differential and a true diagnosis, and what it does to the tools that read the differential from samples. This is a replication and extension in a clinical setting, not a new phenomenon.

The design holds the pretrained weights fixed and varies only the post-training: base/instruct pairs for Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and Mistral-7B; every released stage (SFT, DPO, RL) of OLMo~3 7B~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} and OLMo~3.1 32B, plus a 7B checkpoint trained with RL directly from the base; three medical models fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; and Qwen3-8B with its thinking mode on. Each final pair is also run with worked examples in the prompt, so that base models answer in the requested format.
Our measurements (Appendix~\ref{app:claims} maps each to its evidence and checks):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sampled answers collapse} in every family: post-training costs 13--30 points of pass@32 at each model's best prompt (7--18 after subtracting what samples reach by chance), and most of its first-answer gain disappears with a shared prompt and greedy decoding (\S\ref{sec:sampled}).
\item \textbf{The stated list does not.} It stays close to the base model's view of the patient and varies little between calls; for the smaller Qwen models it names the true diagnosis and the reference's conditions more often, and severe conditions, also from published must-not-miss lists, are named more often on average, though specific ones are lost (\S\ref{sec:stated}).
\item \textbf{Sampling-based methods lose value}: voting stops helping and agreement weakens as an error signal, also on cases where the samples disagree; combining it with the stated probability helps modestly, and thinking mode restores agreement (\S\ref{sec:consequences}).
\item %MEDCASEBULLET
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Post-training and the sampled distribution.}
RL with verifiable rewards improves $\passk{1}$ while base models win at large $k$ on math and code~\citep{yue2025rlboundary,wu2025invisibleleash}; a ``sharpening tax'' appears across 14 base/post-trained pairs on agentic tasks~\citep{oh2026sharpeningtax}, though RL can expand the boundary in domains under-represented in pretraining or with prolonged training~\citep{cheng2025guru,liu2025prorl}, and policy entropy collapses early in RL training~\citep{cui2025entropy}. Instruction tuning and RLHF reduce output diversity~\citep{kirk2024rlhfdiversity,west2025basemodels,jiang2025hivemind,murthy2025onefish}, more so with scale for SFT~\citep{springer2026annotations}, and concentrate next-token distributions so that outputs barely respond to decoding~\citep{yang2026concentration}. Where diversity collapses depends on the pipeline: at DPO in OLMo and OLMo~2~\citep{peeperkorn2025mind} and in a stage ablation by \citet{liu2026alignmenttax}, at each OLMo-2 stage for stated-versus-sampled distributions~\citep{jang2026cannotsample}, and set by training data across Olmo~3's Think, Instruct and RL-Zero lineages~\citep{karouzos2026where}. Base models given a few in-context examples approach aligned models~\citep{lin2023urial}, and theory and experiments cast post-training as sharpening the base model's own distribution toward its mode~\citep{huang2024sharpening,agarwal2025entropy,karan2025sampling}, or as distilling its majority vote~\citep{prasad2025scpo}. Large-$k$ pass@$k$ over few answer options is inflated by chance~\citep{dragoi2025beyondpassk}, and chat templates alone can narrow outputs~\citep{yun2025priceformat}.

\paragraph{Stated versus sampled distributions and uncertainty.}
Models asked to verbalize a distribution match it better than their samples do~\citep{meister2025distributional,jang2026cannotsample}, and they struggle to draw from target distributions directly~\citep{gu2026illusion,zhao2026dice,zhang2024diffuse}; base models are better calibrated than their post-trained versions~\citep{kadavath2022know}. Verbalized sampling keeps most of the base model's diversity through Tülu~3's checkpoints~\citep{zhang2025verbalized}, and aligned answers aggregate content that base models spread across samples on open-ended questions with conflicting perspectives~\citep{lake2025overton}. Our stated readout is the verbalized top-$k$ elicitation of \citet{tian2023justask}. For error detection, sample consistency usually beats verbalized confidence and hybrids beat both~\citep{xiong2024express,lyu2024consistency}, including for diagnosis~\citep{savage2025uncertainty,ren2026medconf}, confidence-weighted voting improves self-consistency~\citep{taubenfeld2025cisc}, and agreement cannot flag errors a model repeats consistently~\citep{tan2025tooconsistent}; aligned models' samples can collapse enough to leave sampling-based uncertainty without signal~\citep{liu2026alignmenttax}, and cross-model disagreement~\citep{hamidieh2026complementing} or the base model~\citep{luo2025pretrained} can supply what self-consistency misses.

\paragraph{Medical models and differential diagnosis.}
LLMs have been evaluated as differential-diagnosis aids on clinicopathological cases~\citep{kanjee2023jama,mcduff2025ddx} and in conversation~\citep{tu2025amie}, and on Phenopacket Store, where they trail the phenotype-driven tool Exomiser~\citep{reese2026phenopacket,chimirri2025rare}; base models have been left out of clinical evaluations for failing to follow instructions~\citep{hager2024clinical}, the format problem our worked examples address. Medical post-training includes verifier-guided SFT and PPO~\citep{chen2024huatuogpto1}, rule-based RL~\citep{liu2025alphamed} and reasoning distillation~\citep{huang2025m1}; RL and reward models tend to favor confident answers~\citep{leng2025taming,damani2025rlcr,mei2025reasoninguncertainty}. DDXPlus scores predicted against reference differentials as sets~\citep{fansi2022casande}; H-DDx proposes hierarchical F1~\citep{h-ddx2025}, MEDDxAgent studies interactive diagnosis~\citep{rose2025meddxagent}, and \citet{puri2026beyondmode} show on DDXPlus with Qwen3-8B that single-answer RL collapses sampled answers and train models to output several answers per completion.
Relative to \citet{jang2026cannotsample}, who compare stated and sampled distributions on synthetic and survey targets with two of our pairs, \citet{puri2026beyondmode} and \citet{karouzos2026where}, we add a clinical reference distribution with a true diagnosis, real case reports scored against their authors' own differentials, must-not-miss conditions taken from published sources, a test of whose per-case view a post-trained list reproduces, prompt, temperature, decoding and chance controls, and the consequences for voting and error signals. Whether a higher temperature closes the gap differs by model: it restores breadth and agreement for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct but not for Qwen3-8B (\S\ref{sec:sampled}).

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Tasks.}
\textbf{DDXPlus}~\citep{fansi2022ddxplus}: synthetic patients from a rule-based medical knowledge base, each with a true pathology and a reference differential, the engine's posterior over 49 conditions. We use 980 test patients (20 per condition) rendered by template as questionnaire-style vignettes without exam or test results (example in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The prompt lists the 49 conditions and asks for the five most likely, with probabilities, as JSON, so this is a closed-set task.
\textbf{MedCaseReasoning}~\citep{wu2025medcasereasoning}: 897 presentations written from open-access case reports (2005--2025), each with the report's final diagnosis and, in its reasoning notes, the alternatives the authors considered. The differential is open-ended; a judge model from a family we do not evaluate on these cases decides whether a name matches the final diagnosis or one of the authors' alternatives, and a second one gives a strict grading (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
\textbf{Phenopacket Store}~\citep{danis2025phenopacketstore}: 1,401 published rare-disease cases (749 diseases) as phenotype vignettes, graded via Mondo (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\paragraph{Models and sampling.}
Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and Mistral-7B-v0.3, each with its official post-trained version; OLMo-3-1025-7B and OLMo-3-1125-32B with the SFT, DPO and final RLVR (Instruct) checkpoints of OLMo~3 7B and OLMo~3.1 32B, plus OLMo-3-7B-RL-Zero-General, trained with RL from the 7B base; and AlphaMed, HuatuoGPT-o1 and m1, fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Table~\ref{tab:models}). We draw 32 samples per case with vLLM at $T{=}0.7$ (top-$p$ 0.95) and, for the 7--8B models, $T{=}1$ (top-$p$ 1), each model with its own chat template (base models without one get a post-trained sibling's) and Qwen3's thinking mode off unless stated. A sample's \emph{top answer} is valid if it is one of the 49 conditions and its \emph{list} if it names at least one; invalid answers count as wrong. With this zero-shot prompt base models are often invalid (up to 47\% of samples for OLMo and 76\% for Mistral), so every final pair is re-run with three worked examples from the training split (Appendix~\ref{sec:fewshot}), on which every model is valid in over 99\% of samples.

\paragraph{Readouts.}
The \emph{sampled} readout is the distribution of top answers over a case's samples: $\passk{k}$~\citep{chen2021codex}, distinct top answers, the reference mass they cover (coverage@32), and total-variation distance (TV) of their empirical distribution to the reference.
The \emph{stated} readout is one sample's list, with its probabilities renormalized over the conditions it names (uniform if missing), averaged per case over valid samples: whether it contains the true pathology among its top $k$ (recall@$k$), its TV to the reference (also with the true pathology removed from both), the reference mass it names, its \emph{conditional} TV (to the reference restricted to the named conditions and renormalized, a scale-free measure of how it allocates probability), DDXPlus's set F1 against the reference's support (DDF1), and whether it names each severe condition (DDXPlus severity 1--2, the dataset's label) that the reference weights at least 0.1.
The \emph{single-call answer} is the top answer of a model's first valid sample.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
DDXPlus has 20 cases per true pathology, so intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from a bootstrap that resamples the 49 pathologies with all their cases (10,000 resamples) unless stated; p-values use the add-one correction. The 112 headline tests (14 metrics fixed in advance, eight zero-shot settings) are primary and Holm-corrected together; every control, stage, few-shot and per-condition analysis is secondary, corrected within its own family (Appendix~\ref{app:clustered}), and read as a check on them. Case-level intervals in the appendix are up to three times narrower, and some appendix tables come from an earlier scorer or a separate script, so values of the same quantity differ by up to 0.006. AUROCs use average ranks for ties. With five to six pipelines, results describe these checkpoints rather than post-training in general.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Post-training collapses the sampled answers}\label{sec:sampled}
Table~\ref{tab:sampled} compares each base model with its post-trained versions. Post-trained models give 1.3--2.4 distinct top answers per 32 samples at $T{=}0.7$, against 3.8--4.9. At 7--8B the post-trained Qwen model is far better on its first answer ($+12.6$ to $+27.5$ points), but its pass@$k$ curve crosses the base model's at $k{\approx}4$--8, and at $k{=}32$ the base model leads by 9.7--14.6 points (Figure~\ref{fig:passk}); at 32B the zero-shot losses are small and not significant. Because base models often answer the zero-shot prompt invalidly, we also compare each base model given worked examples with its post-trained model at its better prompt: post-training then costs 13.2--29.9 points of pass@32 in all six final pairs, including both 32B pairs and Mistral, also with example lists taken from another model instead of the reference (Appendices~\ref{sec:fewshot}--\ref{sec:mistral}). Part of pass@32 is chance: scored against another patient's diagnosis, base models' samples still reach 0.05--0.19 and post-trained ones 0.01--0.04, and after subtracting these the loss with worked examples is 6.7--17.8 points (Appendix~\ref{sec:chance}). Pooled across pairs, the zero-shot loss is 11.5 points $[3.9,19.0]$, with no detectable difference between families (Table~\ref{tab:meta}). Post-trained samples are also farther from the reference than base samples, for three of the five final models farther than a guesser that ignores the patient (TV 0.79--0.85 against 0.774; Table~\ref{tab:stated}).

Two controls separate sharper sampling and prompt format from lost breadth. Sampled hotter, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ matches its base model's pass@32 at $T{=}0.7$, but Qwen3-8B stays below it even at $T{=}1.6$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:controls}). And most of the first-answer gain is prompt format and sampling noise: with the same worked examples the sampled pass@1 gain falls to 1.6--5.2 points in four of six pairs, and decoded greedily, post-training significantly raises top-1 only for Qwen3-8B and Mistral, the two pairs whose few-shot gain stays large (Appendix~\ref{sec:decoding}). Which stage narrows the samples depends on the recipe: RLVR in OLMo~3 7B (19.1 points of pass@32), SFT in OLMo~3.1 32B (12.1), while RL-Zero from the 7B base loses none detectably at $T{=}1$ (Appendices~\ref{sec:stages},~\ref{sec:big32}). Qwen3-8B with thinking on reaches at least its base model's pass@32 (0.815 against 0.732), though its samples stay narrow (1.76 distinct answers; Appendix~\ref{sec:thinking}).

\subsection{The stated list stays close to the base model's view and varies little}\label{sec:stated}
For each patient we compare each post-trained list with the mean of its base model's lists for the same patient (Table~\ref{tab:packing}). In all 15 settings it is closer to that mean than to the base model's means for other patients with the same diagnosis (TV $-0.028$ to $-0.076$), and closer than to other families' base models (by 0.03--0.14). Against every other model's mean for the same patient (Table~\ref{tab:packstrict}), the lists of Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and OLMo~3.1 32B are closest to their own base model's among base models but about as close to the nearest other post-trained model's; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct are not closest to their own base model's. Post-trained lists barely vary between calls (TV 0.10--0.33 to the mean of the model's other lists, against 0.37--0.69 for base lists). For the stronger models, then, a post-trained list is a stabilized version of the base model's view, and the stronger post-trained models converge on one another.

Whether the list improves depends on the model (Table~\ref{tab:stated}). For every Qwen model and OLMo~3.1 32B, one post-trained list contains the true pathology among its top five 12--24 points more often (e.g.\ Qwen3-8B 0.67 $\rightarrow$ 0.80), is closer to the reference (TV $-0.043$ to $-0.088$) and allocates probability among the named conditions more like it (conditional TV $-0.025$ to $-0.078$); OLMo~3 7B's lists do not improve. The gains hold on complete lists, with the true pathology removed and at the full test split's pathology frequencies (Appendices~\ref{sec:statedchecks},~\ref{sec:further}). With worked examples they persist for the smaller Qwen models and Mistral, persist in TV but not recall for Qwen2.5-32B, and weaken for OLMo~3.1 32B; pooled across pairs recall rises in every prompt setting, and the family difference in TV is significant with worked examples ($p{=}0.001$) but not zero-shot (Table~\ref{tab:meta}). Averaging calls closes most of the gap: the mean of five base-model lists is about as close to the reference as one post-trained list (Qwen3-8B 0.699 against 0.700; diamonds in Figure~\ref{fig:stated}a), and with five lists on both sides post-training is closer in only four of eleven settings (Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregate}).

\paragraph{Severe conditions.}
Post-trained lists name the severe conditions that the reference warrants (weight at least 0.1) more often in every zero-shot setting ($+2.2$ to $+13.8$ points, six of eight surviving correction), but one list still names only 0.32--0.47 of them, against 0.975 for the reference's top five, and specific conditions are lost, most often acute pulmonary edema (e.g.\ Qwen2.5-32B 0.63 $\rightarrow$ 0.44; Table~\ref{tab:severetest}), usually in favor of pulmonary embolism or acute coronary syndromes (Appendix~\ref{sec:further}). With must-not-miss conditions taken instead from diagnostic-harm data and the chest-pain guideline~\citep{newmantoker2024burden,gulati2021chestpain}, post-trained lists name 9--24 points more of them in seven of eight zero-shot settings, and only OLMo, Mistral and the few-shot Qwen2.5 models lose specific ones (Appendix~\ref{sec:extmnm}). Asked explicitly for up to three dangerous conditions to rule out, post-trained lists name 6--15 points more of the warranted severe conditions, partly because the lists get longer (0.01--0.11 of the gain remains against two combined standard lists), at a cost of one to two more severe conditions per list that the reference does not warrant (Table~\ref{tab:mnm}). These vignettes lack the ECG and biomarkers that separate the coronary syndromes, so these shifts track the reference engine rather than clinical correctness.

\subsection{Consequences for sampling-based methods}\label{sec:consequences}
\paragraph{Aggregation.}
Majority voting over 32 samples raises base models' accuracy by 3.4--8.4 points at $T{=}0.7$ but adds at most 1.9 for the final post-trained models (Table~\ref{tab:consequences}); models trained to reason before answering (HuatuoGPT-o1, m1) and a chain-of-thought prompt still gain 3.8--4.8 (thinking mode $+1.5$, n.s.), and so does Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ ($+5.6$). The top three of one post-trained Qwen list contains the true pathology 5.6--13.7 points more often than the three most frequent of 32 samples, but only because collapsed samples offer fewer than three answers: matched for size, the list is no better than its samples (Appendix~\ref{sec:further}).

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
We score each signal by how well it ranks the correctness of the single-call answer (AUROC); the stated probability needs no extra calls, \emph{agreement} (the share of 31 further samples with the same top answer) needs them. For base models agreement is the better signal by 0.07--0.26. Post-training weakens it in every family (e.g.\ Qwen3-8B 0.810 $\rightarrow$ 0.655; Figure~\ref{fig:stated}b), by 0.09 $[0.06,0.13]$ pooled, with no detectable difference between families, consistent with \citet{liu2026alignmenttax}. This is not only ties: on cases whose samples are not unanimous, agreement's AUROC is still 0.61--0.66 after post-training against 0.78--0.83 before (Appendix~\ref{sec:further}). Which signal is better then depends on model and prompt: zero-shot, the stated probability wins for Qwen3-8B ($+0.079$ $[0.011,0.142]$) and agreement for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct, and with worked examples the stated probability wins for Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Mistral-Instruct. Thinking returns Qwen3-8B to the base pattern (agreement better by 0.094 $[0.027,0.170]$), and so does sampling Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$. Across 56 model--setting combinations evaluated on held-out cases, a logistic combination of the two signals fitted on 100 labeled cases adds only 0.030 $[0.013,0.044]$ AUROC over agreement for post-trained models (Appendix~\ref{sec:audit}).

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\citet{jang2026cannotsample} show that instruction-tuned models cannot sample from distributions they can describe. In differential diagnosis we find the same split: a post-trained list stays close to the base model's view of the patient and varies far less between calls, so the sampled answers collapse. Evaluations that read the differential from samples (pass@$k$ at large $k$, diversity, self-consistency, agreement-based uncertainty) therefore report a loss that a clinician reading one answer would not see, and error signals that work for base models and reasoning modes work less well for post-trained models answering directly. Post-training adds little that several base-model calls lack: one post-trained call gets about as close to the reference as five averaged base calls, and with a shared prompt and greedy decoding most of its first-answer gain disappears, as the sharpening account predicts~\citep{huang2024sharpening}. Asking for must-not-miss conditions raises their coverage, partly through longer lists, and losses of specific conditions need checking model by model. %MEDCASEDISC
We suggest that studies of diagnostic breadth report which readout they use and whether the model reasons first, and that the error signal be chosen on a small labeled sample from the target population.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
DDXPlus is synthetic and closed-set: its reference differential is a rule-based engine's posterior over questionnaire answers without exam or test results, sometimes clinically implausible (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}), its test set is class-balanced, and the prompt lists all 49 conditions, so our metrics measure agreement with that engine, not clinical correctness, and no clinician reviewed lists or references. Results hold when reweighted to the full test split's pathology frequencies (Appendix~\ref{sec:further}). Severity is the dataset's label or a published list mapped onto its 49 conditions, and the prompt asks for the most likely conditions, not must-not-miss ones. The few-shot controls use one set of example patients, and the temperature controls cover the 7--8B models only. We study open 7--32B models from three families, one reasoning mode and no frontier models. The case-report judges and the extraction of the authors' alternatives are language models not validated against clinicians, and published cases may appear in pretraining data; Phenopacket accuracy is near the floor.

\section{Prompts, vignettes and grading}\label{app:prompts}
\sloppy
\paragraph{DDXPlus.} Each patient's coded evidence is rendered by a fixed template into a vignette of the patient's answers and relevant history; we fix known mistranslations in the English evidence descriptions. An example (test case with true pathology chronic rhinosinusitis; the reference differential gives viral pharyngitis 0.17, chronic rhinosinusitis 0.16, cluster headache 0.15, acute rhinosinusitis 0.14, bronchitis 0.14, URTI 0.12; cluster headache is implausible for this patient, a reminder that the reference is an engine's posterior rather than a clinician's differential):
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
A 13-year-old female presents for evaluation. Symptoms (patient answers): Do you have pain somewhere, related to your reason for consulting? Yes. Characterize your pain: burning. Do you feel pain somewhere? mouth, forehead, cheek(R), cheek(L), nose. How intense is the pain? (scale 0-10) 3. [\dots] Have you lost your sense of smell? Yes. Do you have nasal congestion or a clear runny nose? Yes. Do you have a cough? Yes. Relevant history and risk factors: Have you had a cold in the last 2 weeks? Yes. [\dots] Do you have polyps in your nose? Yes. [\dots]
\end{quote}
System prompt: \texttt{You are an experienced emergency and primary care physician.} The user turn is the vignette followed by: \texttt{Based on the patient's answers above, give your differential diagnosis. Choose ONLY from this list of possible conditions: [the 49 conditions, one per line]. List the 5 most likely conditions from the list, most likely first, with your probability that each is the correct diagnosis. Use the condition names exactly as written in the list. End your answer with a JSON array in exactly this format: [\{"diagnosis": "<name>", "probability": <number between 0 and 1>\}, ...]}. The full-distribution prompt (\S\ref{sec:fullprompt}) instead asks for a probability on every condition, summing to one, most likely first. The plain and CoT variants are described in \S\ref{sec:robust}. Base models receive the same chat-formatted prompt; OLMo base models use their SFT checkpoint's chat template.
\paragraph{Phenopacket Store.} The vignette lists the case's present and (up to ten) excluded HPO phenotypes with sex and age. System prompt: \texttt{You are an expert clinical geneticist and diagnostician.} Instructions: \texttt{List the 5 most likely specific diagnoses (use the specific disease or subtype name, e.g.\ 'Spastic paraplegia 7' rather than 'hereditary spastic paraplegia', when you can), most likely first, with your probability that each is the correct diagnosis}, followed by the same JSON format.
\paragraph{Published case reports.} MedCaseReasoning~\citep{wu2025medcasereasoning} turns open-access PMC case reports (2005--2025) into a presentation, written up to the point where the authors give a differential, and the report's final diagnosis; we use its 897 test cases. The vignette is the presentation as released. System prompt: \texttt{You are an experienced physician and diagnostician.} Instructions: \texttt{Based on the case above, give your differential diagnosis. List the 5 most likely specific diagnoses, most likely first, with your probability that each is the correct diagnosis. End your answer with a JSON array in exactly this format: [\{"diagnosis": "<name>", "probability": <number between 0 and 1>\}, ...]}. Every distinct (case, name) pair among all samples of the eight models, after lower-casing and stripping punctuation, is graded once by Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501 at temperature 0 (the Mistral family is evaluated only on DDXPlus, so no judge grades its own family here): \texttt{Does the candidate name the same disease as the final diagnosis? Count it as the same if it is a synonym, an abbreviation, or a more specific form of the final diagnosis. Count it as different if it is a broader category, a related but distinct disease, or only a complication, cause or finding. Answer with one word: yes or no.} Phi-4, from a family we do not evaluate, grades the same pairs. The first judge's grading is primary, fixed before the results; the \emph{strict} grading, which counts a name as correct only if both judges do, is a sensitivity analysis. Neither judge was validated against clinicians; in a smoke test the first was slightly lenient (it accepted ``Meningioma'' for ``Sclerosing meningioma'').
\paragraph{Grading open-ended names.} Each predicted name is mapped to Mondo by exact lookup against Mondo labels and synonyms (also trying any parenthesized abbreviation and the name without it), then by fuzzy matching (token-sort ratio $\geq 92$). It is \emph{exact} if it maps to the Mondo class of the case's OMIM diagnosis, and \emph{related} if it maps to a specific ancestor group of it (at most two is-a steps up, with at most 150 descendants) or to one of its subtypes; siblings sharing only a broad parent are wrong. The grader passes hand-written checks (e.g.\ ``Hereditary spastic paraplegia type 7 (SPG7)'' is exact for SPG7, ``Hereditary spastic paraplegia'' is related, and Turner syndrome is wrong for Jacobsen syndrome), but we did not validate it against clinician grading, so errors at the scale of the 1--2 point effects we report on this dataset are possible.

\subsection{The sampled differential collapses, while top-1 calibration improves}\label{sec:collapse}
The gap at large $k$ reflects a collapsed \emph{sampled} differential.
Across 32 samples, the post-trained Qwen models name 1.3--2.7 distinct diagnoses per case, against 3.8--8.2 for their base models.
Their answer entropy is 0.11--0.49, against 0.78--1.58 for base models and 1.87 for the reference differential (Table~\ref{tab:pairs}).
Sampling at $T{=}1$ widens the base models' answers but barely changes the post-trained models' (Qwen3 entropy 0.11 at $T{=}0.7$, 0.18 at $T{=}1$), so the collapse lives in the model's own distribution rather than in the decoding settings.
As a result, post-trained models cover less of the reference differential: 0.24 vs.\ 0.41 of its probability mass at $k{=}32$ for Qwen3, and 0.29 vs.\ 0.42 for Qwen2.5.
Their top-1 calibration, however, is markedly better (ECE 0.06 vs.\ 0.21 and 0.14 vs.\ 0.30). This concerns verbalized confidence; alignment is known to worsen the calibration of token probabilities~\citep{openai2023gpt4,zhu2023calibration}, while verbalized confidence of RLHF models is better calibrated~\citep{tian2023justask}.
On every single-answer criterion (accuracy, small-$k$ pass@$k$, calibration), the post-trained model therefore looks strictly better, and the loss of breadth in its samples is invisible. Whether the breadth is lost from the model's stated differential as well is a separate question (\S\ref{sec:stated}).
\paragraph{Which diagnoses drop out?}
For each case and each condition that the reference differential weights at least 0.1, we ask whether the model names that condition as its top answer in at least one of its 32 samples. We call this \emph{considering} the condition.
We group the 49 conditions by DDXPlus severity (1 = most severe).
Post-training lowers consideration in every tier (Table~\ref{tab:severity}), and the severe tier (DDXPlus severity 1--2, the dataset's label rather than a clinical consensus list) is not spared. This metric needs a chance reference, though: a guesser that picks uniformly among the 49 conditions scores about 0.48 on it, and one that draws from the average reference differential 0.56--0.61, above every model, base models included. Considered@32 therefore measures the breadth of the sampled answers, not clinical judgement; we assess severe conditions on the stated list instead (\S\ref{sec:statedchecks}).
For the 17 severity-1--2 conditions (e.g.\ NSTEMI/STEMI, acute pulmonary edema, anaphylaxis, pulmonary embolism, unstable angina), consideration falls from 0.45 to 0.27 for Qwen3 and from 0.51 to 0.34 for Qwen2.5 at $T{=}1$. The differences are $-18.4$ $[-20.8,-15.9]$ and $-17.0$ $[-19.7,-14.2]$ points.
Some diagnoses disappear from the top answer almost entirely.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct names unstable angina as its top answer in one of 107 eligible cases, where its base model does in 47\%. When unstable angina is the true pathology, $\passk{32}$ falls from 1.00 to 0.00. Stable angina (0.89 to 0.06) and myocarditis (0.41 to 0.00) follow the same pattern.
These conditions are not forgotten: they still appear lower in the listed differential (unstable angina in 50\% of eligible cases, against 95\% for the base model). The model ranks them but no longer commits to them.
At the same time, post-trained models concentrate on a few salient ``attractor'' diagnoses.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct gives NSTEMI/STEMI as its top answer in 13.1\% of samples, against a reference rate of 4.4\% and 6.4\% for its base model. It also over-predicts pulmonary embolism (8.2\% vs.\ 3.2\%) and pneumonia (8.0\% vs.\ 2.4\%).
Chest-pain presentations thus collapse onto the most dangerous candidate. That raises its accuracy (NSTEMI $\passk{1}$ 0.99 vs.\ 0.44) at the cost of the alternatives. Ruling out acute coronary syndrome first is standard practice for chest pain, but these vignettes lack the ECG and biomarkers that would separate the coronary conditions, so we read this as a concentration of the samples, not as a clinical error.

\subsection{Robustness to prompt and output format}\label{sec:robust}
\paragraph{Prompt.} We repeat the Qwen3-8B pair with two other prompts (Table~\ref{tab:prompts}). \emph{Plain} drops the system prompt and rewords the instructions. \emph{CoT} asks the model to reason step by step before answering; Qwen3's thinking mode stays off in all runs.
The plain prompt reproduces the pattern: the post-trained model gains $+23.0$ $[+20.9,+25.1]$ points at $k{=}1$ and loses $-7.1$ $[-9.3,-5.0]$ at $k{=}32$ ($T{=}0.7$; at $T{=}1$, $+34.2$ and $-8.7$ $[-11.0,-6.4]$), with 1.3--1.5 distinct answers against 3.7--5.6.
Asking for reasoning changes the picture. With CoT, the post-trained model's $\passk{32}$ rises from 0.607 to 0.835: it leads the base model at every $k$ at $T{=}0.7$ ($+4.7$ $[+2.9,+6.6]$ at $k{=}32$) and ties it at $T{=}1$ ($0.0$ $[-2.1,+1.9]$).
Its samples remain far less diverse than the base model's (2.1--2.2 vs.\ 3.9--6.3 distinct answers), so reasoning recovers coverage at $k{=}32$ without restoring sampling diversity.
The coverage loss is therefore largest when the model answers directly, and reasoning at inference time mitigates it. This matches the medical models: the reasoning-trained descendants widen the differential (\S\ref{sec:medical}).

\paragraph{Output format.} Base models more often give invalid answers: unparsable, or with a top answer outside the 49 conditions. This affects 12\% of Qwen3-8B-Base samples at $T{=}0.7$ and 25\% at $T{=}1$ (Qwen2.5-7B: 1\% and 21\%), against at most 0.3\% for the post-trained models; samples that name no listed condition at all, the stricter definition used in the tables, are 11.3\% and 22.3\% (Qwen2.5-7B: 0.9\% and 18.4\%).
Invalid samples count as wrong, which deflates the base model at large $k$ but inflates the post-trained model's gain at small $k$.
Among valid samples only, the post-trained models still gain $+12.0$ $[+9.8,+14.1]$ (Qwen3, $T{=}0.7$), $+18.7$ $[+16.6,+20.7]$ (Qwen3, $T{=}1$), $+12.2$ $[+10.4,+14.0]$ and $+16.8$ $[+15.0,+18.5]$ points (Qwen2.5) at $k{=}1$, so a quarter to a third of the raw gain at $T{=}1$ is format.
On the cases where every sample of both models is valid, the large-$k$ loss persists: $-15.2$ $[-21.9,-8.6]$ at $k{=}32$ for Qwen3 (105 cases) and $-10.2$ $[-12.7,-7.6]$ for Qwen2.5 (748 cases), both at $T{=}0.7$. (At $T{=}1$ almost no case has 32 valid base samples.) The reported large-$k$ losses are thus lower bounds.

\subsection{Which post-training stage narrows the differential?}\label{sec:stages}
The Qwen pairs compare a base model with the end of a post-training pipeline. OLMo~3~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} releases every stage of its 7B instruct pipeline from one base model: supervised fine-tuning, then DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards on math, code, instruction-following and chat prompts (the released OLMo-3-7B-Instruct). It also releases RL-Zero checkpoints trained with RL directly from the base; we use the general one, trained on chat prompts with an LM-judge reward. (The RL-Zero-Mix checkpoint does not load in our vLLM version.) Table~\ref{tab:stages} gives the results.

The coverage loss replicates in OLMo. At $T{=}0.7$, every post-trained stage is worse than the base at $k{=}32$: by $-14.6$ $[-17.2,-11.6]$ points after SFT, $-9.2$ $[-12.1,-6.1]$ after DPO, $-28.3$ $[-31.4,-25.1]$ after RLVR, and $-13.3$ $[-16.1,-10.5]$ for RL-Zero. Diversity falls at every stage of the pipeline, from 4.93 distinct answers for the base to 4.01 after SFT, 3.30 after DPO and 2.37 after RLVR.

Compared stage to stage, DPO recovers part of what SFT lost at large $k$ ($+5.4$ $[+2.9,+8.1]$ points at $k{=}32$, and $+10.3$ $[+8.9,+11.6]$ at $k{=}1$) while narrowing the differential further ($-0.71$ distinct answers).
The final RLVR stage is the largest single narrowing. Against the DPO model it loses $-19.1$ $[-21.9,-16.5]$ points at $k{=}32$, 0.92 distinct answers and 7.7 points of coverage@32, and it is also worse at $k{=}1$ ($-5.9$ $[-7.1,-4.7]$). It drops severe-tier Considered@32 to 0.25, a loss of $-16.1$ $[-18.6,-13.5]$ points relative to the base, against $-7.4$ for the DPO model. At $T{=}1$ the pattern is the same (RLVR vs.\ DPO: $-12.7$ $[-15.4,-9.9]$ at $k{=}32$).

RL applied directly to the base narrows the differential less. At $T{=}1$, RL-Zero shows no detectable loss of the base model's $\passk{32}$ ($-1.8$ $[-4.4,+0.7]$; clustered $[-5.8,+2.1]$) and 6.28 of its 7.53 distinct answers, and it loses 4.0 $[1.2,6.9]$ points of severe-tier consideration, against 14.4 for the full instruct pipeline. At $T{=}0.7$ it still loses 13.3 points at $k{=}32$ (clustered $[7.3,19.7]$), about as much as SFT.

The OLMo base model often breaks the output format: 40\% of its samples are invalid at $T{=}0.7$ and 47\% at $T{=}1$, while unparsable completions are 16--21\% for the SFT model and at most 1.6\% for the later stages. No case has 32 valid base samples, so the all-valid check of \S\ref{sec:robust} is not possible here. Among valid samples, the post-trained models' gains at $k{=}1$ are small ($-1.9$ to $+4.0$ points at $T{=}0.7$, $+1.3$ to $+8.1$ at $T{=}1$), so most of OLMo's raw gain at $k{=}1$ is format. Invalid samples count as misses and are excluded from distinct answers and entropy, so the base model's coverage and diversity are understated and the losses above are conservative.

Across these pipelines, then, the collapse of the sampled differential is not a necessary effect of RL. It accumulates over the instruction-tuning pipeline; in OLMo~3 7B its largest step is RL applied on top of SFT and DPO, but at 32B it is SFT (\S\ref{sec:big32}), so which stage narrows most depends on the recipe. RL applied directly to a base model (OLMo RL-Zero at $T{=}1$, AlphaMed's base-initialized checkpoint, and our GRPO runs in \S\ref{sec:training}) narrows the differential less, but not always: RL-Zero at $T{=}0.7$ loses about as much as SFT.
On Phenopacket Store the instruct pipeline also loses coverage: every stage trails the base model at $k{=}32$ ($-2.0$ $[-3.0,-1.1]$ points after SFT, $-1.5$ after DPO, $-1.4$ after RLVR; all significant), while RL-Zero does not ($-0.6$ $[-1.4,+0.2]$), from a base model at 4.5\% (\S\ref{sec:rare}).

\subsection{Replication at 32B}\label{sec:big32}
We repeat the DDXPlus comparisons at $T{=}0.7$ with Qwen2.5-32B and its Instruct model, and with every released stage of OLMo~3.1 32B, whose base is OLMo-3-1125-32B; there is no 32B RL-Zero checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:big32}).
The sampled differential collapses at 32B too, by about half as much. Post-training costs 4.9 $[2.9,7.1]$ points at $\passk{32}$ for Qwen2.5-32B and 4.0 $[2.0,6.0]$ for OLMo~3.1 32B (clustered $[-0.5,10.3]$ and $[1.2,6.8]$; neither survives correction), halves the number of distinct answers (3.78 $\rightarrow$ 1.68 and 4.32 $\rightarrow$ 2.15), and lowers severe-tier consideration by 6.1 and 8.4 points. For Qwen the loss holds on the 421 cases where every base sample is valid ($-4.8$ $[-7.8,-1.7]$); the OLMo 32B base gives invalid answers on 27\% of samples. With few-shot prompts, which nearly remove invalid answers, both losses are three to five times larger (\S\ref{sec:fewshot}).
The stage attribution differs from 7B. In OLMo~3.1 32B the largest narrowing is SFT ($\passk{32}$ $-12.1$ $[-14.5,-9.8]$, clustered $[-18.0,-6.8]$, distinct answers $-1.39$, severe tier $-11.9$); DPO recovers most of it ($+10.9$ against SFT), and the final RL step is small ($-2.8$ $[-4.4,-1.2]$ at $k{=}32$, clustered $[-5.3,-0.5]$, $-0.14$ distinct answers). OLMo~3.1 32B and OLMo~3 7B were post-trained with different recipes, so the stage that narrows the samples most is a property of the pipeline, not of SFT, DPO or RL as such.
Under this prompt the stated readout improves in both 32B families (\S\ref{sec:stated}; with few-shot prompts only Qwen2.5-32B's does robustly, \S\ref{sec:fewshot}), and voting stops paying off: majority voting over 32 samples gains the base models 7.0 and 8.4 points and the Instruct models 1.9 and 0.9 (the latter n.s.).

\subsection{Ruling out format, accuracy and floor effects}\label{sec:statedchecks}
Table~\ref{tab:robust} tests simpler explanations of the stated-list result. For reference, one list's recall@5 rises after post-training by 13.6, 16.2 and 12.0 points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B at $T{=}0.7$ and by 21.9 and 24.0 for the 7--8B Qwen models at $T{=}1$, by 12.7 for OLMo~3.1 32B, and for OLMo~3 7B by 1.5 (n.s.) at $T{=}0.7$ and 10.0 at $T{=}1$ (condition-clustered intervals exclude zero except where marked).
\emph{Format.} The general models' post-trained lists and base-model lists at $T{=}0.7$ are nearly all complete (81--99\% name five on-list conditions with probabilities; Qwen base models at $T{=}1$, 61--62\%; the medical models, 61--80\%). Restricting to complete lists leaves the gaps unchanged (Qwen2.5-7B: 0.779 vs.\ 0.719).
\emph{Accuracy.} A more accurate model puts more stated mass on the true pathology, which the reference also favors. Within samples whose top answer is correct, and within those whose top answer is wrong, the post-trained Qwen list is still closer to the reference ($-0.018$ to $-0.034$ and $-0.036$ to $-0.079$ in TV across both temperatures; Table~\ref{tab:robust} shows $T{=}0.7$).
\emph{Floor.} One list can do no better than the reference's own top five, and a finite set of samples no better than as many draws from the reference. Measured as excess over these floors, post-training lowers the list's TV (Qwen3-8B 0.479 $\rightarrow$ 0.435) and raises the samples' (0.557 $\rightarrow$ 0.613).
\emph{True pathology removed.} With the true pathology removed from both list and reference and both renormalized, the post-trained Qwen and OLMo~3.1 32B lists are still closer to the reference ($-0.018$ to $-0.056$), and DDXPlus's set F1 against the reference's support improves by 0.040--0.083 (Table~\ref{tab:clustered}). TV, mass and DDF1 are not independent, since a list's TV is the reference mass it leaves out plus the mass it misallocates among the conditions it names, and the second term grows mechanically with the first. The conditional TV, between the list and the reference restricted to the named conditions and renormalized, removes this: it falls after post-training for every Qwen pair and OLMo~3.1 32B ($-0.025$ to $-0.078$, all surviving correction) and rises for OLMo~3 7B at $T{=}1$ ($+0.064$), so the Qwen lists both name better conditions and weight them more like the reference.
\emph{Patient-blind list.} A fixed list of the five conditions with the highest average reference probability scores TV 0.794, mass 0.238 and DDF1 0.272. Every model's list beats it in TV except OLMo~3 7B's and Qwen2.5-7B's base list at $T{=}1$; in DDF1 several base lists do not (Qwen2.5-7B 0.245, OLMo~3 7B 0.225), and the post-trained Qwen and OLMo~3.1 32B lists exceed it by 0.035--0.061.
\emph{Over-naming.} As a share of named conditions, post-trained lists name fewer that the reference gives less than 1\% (Qwen3-8B 0.59 $\rightarrow$ 0.55) and fewer unwarranted severe conditions (Qwen2.5-32B 0.74 $\rightarrow$ 0.65), but as a count per list the change is mixed: from 0.22 fewer unwarranted severe conditions (OLMo~3.1 32B) to 0.35 more (OLMo~3 7B).
\emph{Severe conditions one by one.} Table~\ref{tab:severetest} tests each of the 17 severe conditions, counting it wherever the reference gives it any weight. After Benjamini--Hochberg correction, 2--7 conditions per zero-shot pair are named significantly less often after post-training and 0--6 significantly more often, and no pair names more conditions less often than a random swap of base and post-trained lists would. \subsection{Few-shot elicitation}\label{sec:fewshot}
The main analyses give every model the same zero-shot chat prompt, which base models were not trained to follow; they give invalid answers on up to 47\% of samples, which we count as wrong. We therefore re-ran the five final base/post-trained pairs at $T{=}0.7$ with three worked examples in the prompt: three fixed DDXPlus training patients with different true pathologies, each followed by the top five conditions of its reference differential, with probabilities, in the JSON format the instructions ask for. Base models see the instructions and examples as a plain document and complete the next one (generation stops at the end of the list); post-trained models see the same instructions and examples as earlier chat turns. Invalid samples fall below 0.5\% for every model (Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}).

The examples help base models most. Their pass@1 rises from 0.178 to 0.306 for OLMo-3-1025-7B, from 0.326 to 0.528 for OLMo-3-1125-32B and from 0.500 to 0.647 for Qwen2.5-32B, and their pass@32 reaches 0.76--0.90, while their stated lists barely change (TV 0.712--0.787, against 0.726--0.794 zero-shot). The losses in the samples therefore grow (Table~\ref{tab:fewshotdiff}): post-training costs 15.2--29.9 points of pass@32 in every pair, including both 32B pairs, whose zero-shot losses did not survive correction, and every post-trained model's Cov@32 (0.21--0.25) is below every base model's (0.33--0.45) and below a uniform guesser's.

For the three Qwen pairs the stated-list improvement persists and mostly grows: TV $-0.057$ to $-0.087$, $-0.055$ to $-0.073$ with the true pathology removed, and DDF1 $+0.051$ to $+0.081$, all surviving correction. OLMo~3 7B remains the exception (TV $+0.021$, n.s.; $+0.024$ without the true pathology). For OLMo~3.1 32B the improvement shrinks: the post-trained list still places more reference mass on the conditions it names ($+0.047$), but its TV ($-0.022$), TV without the true pathology ($-0.006$) and DDF1 ($+0.009$) no longer survive correction. At 32B the stated-list result is therefore robust for Qwen2.5-32B but depends on the prompt for OLMo~3.1 32B. Post-trained lists still name 5--10 of the 17 severe conditions less often than their base model's.

The consequences replicate in all five pairs. Majority voting over 32 samples gains base models 6.2--10.9 points and post-trained models 0.2--1.1. The three most frequent of 32 sampled answers beat the top three of one stated list by 8.2--22.0 points for base models, while for post-trained models the list is ahead by 0.2--16.7. Agreement with four more samples is the better error signal for every base model (AUROC 0.79--0.84, against 0.67--0.73 for the stated probability), and the stated probability for every post-trained model (0.69--0.77, against 0.56--0.68); against agreement across 31 samples the stated probability wins clearly only for Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ($+0.108$ and $+0.130$, clustered) and ties for the others.
This check uses one temperature and one set of examples, and the examples show the reference's own lists, which may help every model match the reference.

\subsection{Two further controls}\label{sec:controls}
\paragraph{Reference-free few-shot examples.} The examples of \S\ref{sec:fewshot} show each training patient's reference top five, and so also the target of the evaluation. We re-ran the six final pairs with the same three patients but with each example list replaced by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct's own greedy answer (on-list, with the true pathology first for two patients and second for one). The conclusions do not change (Table~\ref{tab:refree}). Post-training costs 12.7--29.7 points of pass@32 in every pair. The stated list moves closer to the reference for the three Qwen pairs and, now clearly, for Mistral (TV $-0.053$ to $-0.077$; $-0.031$ to $-0.059$ with the true pathology removed), but not for OLMo~3 7B or OLMo~3.1 32B, and wherever the list improves its allocation among the named conditions also moves closer to the reference (conditional TV $-0.051$ to $-0.100$). Voting helps base models by 3.7--13.7 points and post-trained ones by at most 1.4; the top three of one list beats the three most frequent samples for every post-trained model; the stated probability beats agreement as an error signal for every post-trained model and agreement beats it for every base model; and no pair names more severe conditions less often than chance. Five of six base models score lower at $k{=}1$ than with reference-derived examples (OLMo-3-1125-32B 0.453 against 0.528).

\paragraph{Matched temperature.} Post-trained models are sharper than their base models at the same nominal temperature, so part of the narrowing could be colder effective sampling. We sampled Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ and 1.6 (Table~\ref{tab:matcht}), and the two models differ. Qwen3-8B barely broadens: at $T{=}1.6$ it gives 1.92 distinct answers and reaches 0.684 at pass@32, still below its base model at $T{=}0.7$ (0.732) and at $T{=}1$ (0.789; $-10.5$ points, surviving correction); voting gains stay under 2 points, agreement stays weaker than for the base model, and its stated list does not change (TV 0.701). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct does broaden: at $T{=}1.3$, with 2\% invalid answers, it gives 3.38 distinct answers, matches its base model's pass@32 at $T{=}0.7$ (0.704 against 0.695), gains 5.6 points from voting, and agreement is again the better error signal (0.794 against 0.602), at a cost of 4.2 points of pass@1; at $T{=}1.6$, 31\% of its answers are invalid. Its stated list stays closer to the reference than the base model's (0.722 against 0.779). For some models, then, much of the sampled narrowing is sharper sampling that a higher temperature undoes; the stated-list result is not.

\subsection{Greedy decoding, temperature frontier and a must-not-miss prompt}\label{sec:decoding}
\paragraph{Greedy single call.} Deployed systems often decode greedily. We compared one greedy answer per case under three prompt pairings (Table~\ref{tab:greedy}): both arms with the reference-derived worked examples of \S\ref{sec:fewshot} (\emph{same examples}); base models with the reference-free examples of Table~\ref{tab:refree} against zero-shot post-trained models, so that neither arm sees a reference list (\emph{reference-free base}); and reference-example base models against zero-shot post-trained models, the pairing we first ran. Under every pairing post-training significantly raises top-1 in at most two of six pairs. With the same examples these are Qwen3-8B ($+13.7$) and Mistral ($+10.8$); Qwen2.5-7B gains $+1.8$ and the other three lose 4.8--5.8 points, all n.s. With reference-free base models only Qwen3-8B gains ($+10.7$), while Qwen2.5-32B ($-8.0$) and OLMo~3 7B ($-14.5$) lose significantly. The example lists matter little to the base models (top-1 within 2.7 points, except OLMo~3.1 32B, 0.630 against 0.559) but raise five of the six post-trained models (Mistral 0.417 to 0.577, OLMo~3 7B 0.258 to 0.345; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct falls to 0.458). Under every pairing the two smaller Qwen models' lists contain the true pathology more often (recall@5 $+16.8$ to $+26.0$) and name more warranted severe conditions, every Qwen list is closer to the reference, and OLMo~3 7B's lists are farther. Together with the few-shot comparison, where the sampled pass@1 gain is already only 1.6--5.2 points in four of six pairs, most of post-training's advantage at one sampled call for the 7--8B Qwen models ($+12.6$ to $+27.5$ points at $T{=}0.7$ and 1, \S\ref{sec:sampled}) is therefore prompt format and the base model's sampling noise; Qwen3-8B and Mistral keep a real gain.

\paragraph{Temperature frontier.} With worked examples and top-$p$ fixed at 1, we sampled the three 7--8B pairs at $T = 0.4$, 0.7, 1.0 and 1.3 (Table~\ref{tab:frontier}). Post-trained pass@1 barely moves with temperature (Qwen3-8B 0.60, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct 0.43--0.46, OLMo-3-7B-Instruct 0.29--0.35), pass@32 rises (to 0.70, 0.66 and 0.74 at $T{=}1.3$), and the stated list barely changes (TV 0.68, 0.70 and 0.81--0.83), consistent with the concentration described by \citet{yang2026concentration}. Base models trade pass@1 (Qwen3-8B-Base 0.45 to 0.18) for pass@32, which peaks at $T{=}1$ or 1.3 (0.85--0.88), and 25--36\% of their answers are invalid at $T{=}1.3$. The frontiers differ by family. For Qwen3-8B the post-trained model is better at pass@1 and the base model at pass@32 at every temperature tested; for Qwen2.5-7B the curves meet (at pass@1 about 0.43, the base model at $T{=}0.4$ and the Instruct model at $T{=}1.3$ both reach pass@32 0.66); and for OLMo~3 7B the base model at $T{=}0.4$ beats its Instruct model at every temperature on both (0.368 and 0.803, against at best 0.345 and 0.737). Agreement becomes a better error signal for post-trained models as temperature rises (Qwen3-8B 0.57 to 0.68) but stays below the base models' (0.81--0.92).

\paragraph{Must-not-miss prompt.} The standard prompt asks for the most likely conditions. We asked the six post-trained models for the five most likely conditions and then up to three dangerous conditions to rule out (Table~\ref{tab:mnm}). Whole lists then name 0.44--0.58 of the severe conditions that the reference weights at least 0.1, 6--15 points more than with the standard prompt and 11--21 more than the base models' lists, and acute pulmonary edema is recovered for every model but Qwen3-8B (e.g. Qwen2.5-32B 0.44 $\rightarrow$ 0.63, OLMo~3 7B 0.31 $\rightarrow$ 0.58). The cost is 1.1--2.1 more unwarranted severe conditions per list, mostly pulmonary embolism, named in 88--100\% of eligible cases, and for Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Mistral a shorter most-likely part that contains the true pathology 5--10 points less often. Even when asked, no model names more than 0.58 of the warranted severe conditions.

\subsection{Must-not-miss conditions defined outside DDXPlus}\label{sec:extmnm}
The severity label used above is the dataset's own. We therefore re-ran every severe-condition analysis with two published sets of conditions that must not be missed, mapped onto DDXPlus's 49 (Table~\ref{tab:extmnm}): a harm-based set, the DDXPlus conditions among the 15 diseases that cause about half of serious misdiagnosis-related harms in the USA~\citep{newmantoker2024burden} (NSTEMI/STEMI, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia and pulmonary neoplasm, of which DDXPlus labels only the first two severe), and the life-threatening causes of acute chest pain in the 2021 AHA/ACC guideline~\citep{gulati2021chestpain} (acute coronary syndrome as NSTEMI/STEMI and unstable angina, pulmonary embolism, esophageal rupture as Boerhaave, and pneumothorax). Their union has seven conditions. Neither set involves a judgment of these vignettes, and both were fixed before the analysis.
On average the result is stronger than with DDXPlus's labels. Zero-shot, post-trained lists name 9--24 points more of the warranted union conditions in seven of eight settings (OLMo~3.1 32B $-0.3$, n.s.), and with worked examples 7--22 points more for the three Qwen models and OLMo~3 7B (OLMo~3.1 32B $+6.5$ and Mistral $+4.0$, n.s.); the harm-based and chest-pain sets give the same zero-shot pattern. The losses of specific conditions, however, are confined to OLMo, Mistral and the few-shot Qwen2.5 models. No Qwen model names any union condition significantly less often zero-shot, while OLMo~3 7B loses NSTEMI/STEMI and unstable angina at both temperatures (and pulmonary neoplasm at $T{=}1$), OLMo~3.1 32B loses NSTEMI/STEMI, pulmonary embolism and pneumothorax, and with worked examples Mistral loses four of the seven (pulmonary embolism 31 points), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct two and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct one (Boerhaave). When the true pathology is itself in the union set (140 cases), zero-shot post-trained lists contain it among their top five 10--26 points more often for four of five pairs (OLMo~3 7B $+9.9$, n.s.). Scored against the union set, the must-not-miss prompt names 15--26 points more of the warranted conditions than the standard prompt and 8--20 points more than two combined standard lists, a larger share of its gain than under DDXPlus's labels, at a cost of 0.7--1.5 more unwarranted union conditions per list.

\subsection{Chance-corrected pass@32 and other models' views}\label{sec:chance}
\paragraph{Chance.} With 49 listed conditions, part of pass@32 comes from answers that do not depend on the patient. We therefore also score each case's samples against the true pathology of a random case with a different pathology (Table~\ref{tab:chance}). Base models' samples then still reach 0.05--0.19 at pass@32 and post-trained ones 0.01--0.04, far below the 0.48 of a uniform guesser, because both concentrate on conditions that fit the presentation. Subtracting these values shrinks the loss but keeps it in every pair with worked examples: 6.7--17.8 points with the reference-derived examples and 4.1--14.3 with another model's. Zero-shot, the corrected loss is 7.5 and 3.6 points for the 7--8B Qwen models and 25.2 for OLMo~3 7B, and it disappears at 32B ($-0.5$ and $+0.1$).

\paragraph{Other models' views.} The packing test of Table~\ref{tab:packing} compares a post-trained list with its base model's mean for the same patient against the base model's means for other patients with the same diagnosis, a lenient baseline that any list responding to the patient passes. Against other families' base models alone, every zero-shot post-trained list is closer to its own base model's mean ($-0.031$ to $-0.138$). Table~\ref{tab:packstrict} compares it with every other final model's mean for the same patient. For Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and OLMo~3.1 32B the post-trained list is closer to its own base model's view than to any other base model's (by 0.036--0.058) and far closer than to the average post-trained model's (0.067--0.090), but about as close to the nearest other post-trained model's (n.s.). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is as close to Qwen3-8B-Base's view as to its own base model's, and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct is closer to Qwen3-8B-Base's and Qwen3-8B's. For the stronger models, then, a post-trained list stays close to its own base model's view, while the stronger post-trained models also converge on one another.

\subsection{Ties, list size, validity, prevalence and the reference ceiling}\label{sec:further}
These checks use the zero-shot $T{=}0.7$ final pairs.
\emph{Ties.} Post-trained models' 32 samples are unanimous in 34--57\% of cases (Qwen3-8B 78\%), where agreement cannot rank anything. On the remaining cases agreement's AUROC is 0.61--0.66 for the post-trained models against 0.78--0.83 for their base models, so agreement weakens even where it is not tied. On those cases Qwen3-8B's stated probability no longer beats agreement (0.611 against 0.617): its advantage comes from cases where all samples agree, whereas OLMo-3-7B-Instruct's stated probability stays ahead (0.729 against 0.605).
\emph{List size.} The three most frequent of 32 samples can be fewer than three answers. With $m = \min(3, \text{distinct sampled answers})$, the first $m$ items of one list contain the true pathology about as often as the $m$ most frequent samples for the post-trained models ($-0.5$ to $-5.2$ points; significantly lower for Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct), while for base models the samples win by 8.5--14.6. The advantage of a post-trained list's top three is that it names three conditions where its samples offer fewer.
\emph{Validity.} Counting invalid samples as wrong (as everywhere in the paper) or scoring valid samples only leaves pass@32 unchanged and moves pass@1, mostly for OLMo~3 7B, whose single-answer gain is $+7.1$ points with invalid samples counted as wrong and $-3.5$ (n.s.) over valid samples only, weighted to the natural prevalence below.
\emph{Prevalence.} The test sample is class-balanced (20 cases per pathology). Reweighted to the pathology frequencies of the full DDXPlus test split (134,529 patients), post-training costs 4.9--31.5 points of pass@32 (all intervals exclude zero, including both 32B pairs), raises recall@5 by 10.3--17.7 points for the Qwen models and OLMo~3.1 32B ($-0.3$ for OLMo~3 7B) and lowers one list's TV by 0.047--0.069 (OLMo~3 7B $+0.031$).
\emph{Reference ceiling.} The reference's own top five contains the true pathology in 96.9\% of cases (97.3\% at natural prevalence), against 0.47--0.84 for one model list.
\emph{What replaces acute pulmonary edema.} On the 54 cases whose reference gives acute pulmonary edema at least 0.1, the conditions whose share of lists rises most after post-training are mostly other must-not-miss conditions (pulmonary embolism for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, $+0.29$ and $+0.52$; pneumonia and pulmonary embolism for Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct; unstable angina, panic attack and NSTEMI/STEMI for Qwen3-8B), but atrial fibrillation and influenza for OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct, whose lists name acute pulmonary edema 0.25 less often.

\subsection{A third family: Mistral-7B v0.3}\label{sec:mistral}
We repeat the comparison with Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, a family also studied by \citet{jang2026cannotsample}. With the zero-shot prompt the base model does not answer: 52\% of its samples at $T{=}0.7$ and 76\% at $T{=}1$ are invalid (it continues the document instead), and its valid answers mostly repeat the first listed condition, so its single-call accuracy is 6\% and the zero-shot pair is not informative. With the few-shot prompt of \S\ref{sec:fewshot} the base model answers validly (0.4\% invalid), and the pair reproduces the other families (Table~\ref{tab:third}). Post-training raises pass@1 by 14.0 points and lowers pass@32 by 14.5, cuts distinct answers from 5.0 to 1.3 and Cov@32 by 0.138, and moves the samples away from the reference (TV $+0.058$). Voting gains the base model 5.6 points and the Instruct model 0.8; the three most frequent of 32 samples beat one list by 12.6 points for the base model, while for the Instruct model the list is ahead by 7.0; agreement is the better error signal for the base model (AUROC 0.826 against 0.671) and the stated probability for the Instruct model (0.747 against 0.613). The stated list improves modestly (TV $-0.036$ and DDF1 $+0.046$, surviving clustering and Holm correction over 42 tests; $-0.023$ with the true pathology removed, which does not), the smallest significant gain of the three families, and 11 of the 17 severe conditions are named less often (3 significantly). Mistral-Instruct also names 0.5 conditions per list that are not among the 49.

\section{Thinking mode, aggregation and the error signal}\label{app:consequences}
\subsection{Thinking mode}\label{sec:thinking}
Qwen3-8B can reason in a thinking block before it answers; the main runs turn this off. With thinking on (zero-shot prompt, $T{=}0.7$, up to 8,192 tokens; traces average about 1,500 tokens and all end before the limit), the same weights behave differently (Table~\ref{tab:third}). Accuracy rises at both ends: pass@1 is 0.671 against 0.559 without thinking, and pass@32 is 0.815, above the non-thinking model (0.607) and level with the base model (0.732 zero-shot, $+8.4$ $[1.7,15.4]$ points, not surviving Holm correction over 42 tests; 0.809 with worked examples). The samples are still narrow (1.76 distinct answers against 3.77 for the base model; Cov@32 0.283 against 0.323) but no farther from the reference than the base model's (TV 0.745 against 0.750). The stated list is the closest of any 7--8B model (TV 0.670, against 0.700 without thinking). The shift in error signals reverses: agreement across 31 samples is again the better signal (AUROC 0.732 against 0.638; difference 0.094 $[0.027,0.170]$), as for base models, and voting gains 1.5 points (n.s.). The narrowing of the samples and the advantage of the stated probability are therefore properties of answering directly, which a reasoning mode largely undoes, consistent with the chain-of-thought prompt of \S\ref{sec:robust}.

\subsection{Averaging stated lists across calls}\label{sec:aggregate}
Stated lists can be averaged across calls, as samples can be voted. For each case we average the first 5 or 32 valid stated lists, each renormalized over the conditions it names, and score the mean against the reference (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}). Averaging helps base models far more than post-trained ones, whose lists vary little between calls (at $T{=}0.7$ with the zero-shot prompt, 32 averaged lists name 7.6--13.4 conditions, against 16.6--22.0 for base models). The mean of five base-model lists is about as close to the reference as one post-trained list (Qwen3-8B 0.699 against 0.700, Qwen2.5-7B 0.717 against 0.717, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.691 against 0.701). With five lists on both sides, the post-trained mean is closer for Qwen2.5-7B under both prompts ($-0.025$ and $-0.027$) and for Qwen2.5-32B and Qwen3-8B under one prompt each, not significantly closer for OLMo~3.1 32B (zero-shot) and Mistral (few-shot), and farther for OLMo~3 7B and few-shot OLMo~3.1 32B. Averaged base lists also name severe conditions at least as often, and more often for OLMo~3.1 32B, OLMo~3 7B, Mistral and few-shot Qwen2.5-32B (by 0.06--0.14). One post-trained call thus gets about as close to the reference as several base calls; the packing test (Table~\ref{tab:packing}) shows that it is a stabilized version of the base model's view of the patient rather than its literal average, and, as for the open-ended questions of \citet{lake2025overton}, post-training adds little that averaged base lists lack (five post-trained lists beat five base lists in four of eleven settings). Qwen3-8B's thinking mode goes furthest: five of its lists beat five base-model lists by more than any direct-answer model's (TV $-0.049$) and name more severe conditions ($+0.129$). For a post-trained model answering directly, averaging five lists instead of reading one improves TV by only 0.005--0.025 at $T{=}0.7$ (up to 0.038 at $T{=}1$); it names 6--17 points more severe conditions, mostly by naming 1.4 to 3.6 more conditions. Five post-trained lists still cover 0.44--0.50 of the reference mass at $T{=}0.7$ (OLMo~3 7B 0.37) with six to eight conditions, about twice what their 32 samples cover (0.22--0.28) and as much as a uniform guesser naming about 24 conditions (0.47), so the coverage deficit of \S\ref{sec:sampled} is one of the sampled readout.

\subsection{Which signal to use: combination and held-out audit}\label{sec:audit}
Whether the stated probability or agreement across samples is the better error signal varies across models, so we ask what predicts it and whether a practitioner can tell. We pool 56 model--setting combinations scored on DDXPlus: the base/post-trained pairs at both temperatures and with three prompts, the OLMo stages, the 32B models, the medical models (including three fine-tuned from Llama-3.1-8B, HuatuoGPT-o1-8B, AlphaMed-8B and UltraMedical-8B, which appear only here), our GRPO runs, and the full-distribution prompt. They share weights, prompts and temperatures, so we treat the correlations below as descriptive.
The stated probability's advantage over five-sample agreement correlates with the \emph{single-answer rate}, the share of cases whose five samples all give the same top answer (Spearman 0.75), with the stated probability's own AUROC (0.82; the gap contains this term, so part of this is by construction), and negatively with the number of distinct answers ($-0.74$). The stated probability is the better signal for 19 of the 56. No single-answer threshold separates them: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct still favors agreement, while OLMo~3 7B's DPO and Instruct models favor their unusually informative stated probability.
With five-sample agreement, as in the original audit, combining the signals beats choosing between them; with agreement across all 31 further samples the advantage shrinks (post-trained combinations: agreement 0.742, rank average 0.761, $+0.019$ $[-0.007,0.040]$; logistic 0.772, $+0.030$ $[0.013,0.044]$), and for the 17 base combinations agreement alone (0.821) beats the rank average (0.780) and ties the logistic combination (0.827). The rest of this section uses five-sample agreement. Fitting a logistic model of correctness on the stated probability and five-sample agreement with 100 labeled cases, and evaluating on the other 880, gives a mean held-out AUROC of 0.753 for the 39 post-trained combinations (0.765 for all 56); averaging the two signals' ranks without fitting gives 0.746; picking the better single signal on the same 100 cases gives 0.720; always using agreement 0.695; always using the stated probability 0.671. The logistic combination beats the single-signal pick for 35 of the 39 post-trained combinations. This matches the general finding that hybrids of consistency and verbalized confidence beat either~\citep{xiong2024express}.
For choosing a single signal, the simplest rule, always using agreement, is right for 66\% of the combinations (54\% of the 39 post-trained ones) and loses 0.025 AUROC on average (0.032). An audit does better: choose the signal with the higher AUROC on 100 random labeled cases, using five samples per case, and evaluate the choice on the other 880. Over 500 draws per combination it picks the better held-out signal 86\% of the time (82\% for post-trained models) and loses 0.005 AUROC (0.007); with 50 cases, 82\% and 0.010; with 200, 90\% and 0.003. It is reliable when the two signals differ by more than 0.05 AUROC (96\% of draws, 39 combinations) and close to a coin flip when they differ by less than 0.02 (53\%, 5 combinations), where the choice matters least. The audit's main cost is gold labels for 100 cases from the target population, and we validate it only within DDXPlus.


\section{Medical post-training and rare disease}\label{app:medrare}
\subsection{Medical post-training}\label{sec:medical}
Applied after instruction tuning, medical RL does not cost coverage. AlphaMed improves on its parent at every $k$ (Figure~\ref{fig:alphamed}): +3.9 points at $k{=}1$ ($[+2.2,+5.5]$; clustered $[-0.4,+8.4]$), widening to +8.4 at $k{=}32$ ($[+6.1,+10.6]$; clustered $[+2.6,+14.7]$). It reaches at least one correct sample for 47 of 49 conditions, against 39 for its parent.
It does not restore breadth, though: distinct answers (1.87 vs.\ 1.82), entropy (0.28 vs.\ 0.30), and coverage (0.27 vs.\ 0.24) stay at the instruction-tuned level, far below the base model.
AlphaMed also names off-list conditions more often than its parent, a mild loss of instruction following.
By condition, AlphaMed slightly \emph{raises} consideration in every severity tier (+2.2 to +4.3 points; Table~\ref{tab:severity}). It still loses acute pulmonary edema (0.31 to 0.13), and it introduces an attractor of its own: spontaneous pneumothorax as the top answer in 10.3\% of samples, against 4.3\% for its parent and a reference rate of 1.0\%.
Medical post-training thus operates on an already collapsed distribution; the collapse comes from the general post-training stage.
The other two descendants go further (Table~\ref{tab:medical}). HuatuoGPT-o1 (SFT then PPO) and m1 (SFT distillation from a reasoning teacher) both \emph{widen} the differential relative to their parent: 2.49 and 3.29 distinct answers vs.\ 1.82, and coverage@32 of 0.31 and 0.33 vs.\ 0.24. Their pass@32 rises by 13.7 $[+11.3,+16.0]$ and 18.1 $[+15.4,+20.8]$ points (clustered $[8.0,20.0]$ and $[10.5,25.9]$). m1 gains almost nothing at $k{=}1$ (+0.9, interval includes zero), so its improvement lies entirely in breadth. Both reasoning-trained models are also less calibrated (ECE 0.17 and 0.21 vs.\ 0.11). In these three models, medical post-training on an instruction-tuned model does not add to the collapse, and reasoning distillation partially reverses it.

\paragraph{Medical RL from the base model.}
Alongside the instruct-initialized AlphaMed-7B, the AlphaMed authors released a companion checkpoint, AlphaMed-7B-base-rl, initialized from Qwen2.5-7B rather than Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (according to its configuration file). The paper~\citep{liu2025alphamed} describes only the instruct-initialized models: GRPO with a binary correctness reward on about 19k multiple-choice questions from MedQA and MedMCQA, with a 64-question by 8-sample batch for 300 steps. It does not document the base-initialized checkpoint, so we cannot rule out differences in data or hyperparameters, and we treat it as corroborating rather than controlled evidence.
It offers a third-party check on whether RL itself causes the collapse (Table~\ref{tab:medical}, lower block).
It does not. RL from the base model improves on the base at every $k$ ($+14.3$ $[+13.1,+15.7]$ points at $k{=}1$, $+9.5$ $[+7.4,+11.6]$ at $k{=}32$). It keeps 3.14 distinct answers, against 1.87 for the instruct-initialized AlphaMed.
Against that instruct-model AlphaMed, it is 2.2 $[0.4,3.9]$ points worse at $k{=}1$ and 10.8 $[+8.6,+13.2]$ points better at $k{=}32$.
Its severe-tier Considered@32 shows no detectable change relative to the base ($-0.7$ $[-3.0,+1.7]$ points), against $-12.4$ for instruction tuning and $-8.1$ for instruction tuning followed by AlphaMed's RL (Table~\ref{tab:severity}; all well below the chance levels of \S\ref{sec:collapse}).
RL from a base model also narrows less in OLMo's RL-Zero at $T{=}1$ and in our GRPO runs (\S\ref{sec:training}), but not uniformly: RL-Zero at $T{=}0.7$ loses about as much as SFT, and within OLMo~3 7B's instruct pipeline the RL stage after SFT and DPO is the largest single narrowing (\S\ref{sec:stages}).

\subsection{Rare disease: overconfidence without accuracy}\label{sec:rare}
On 1,401 Phenopacket Store cases covering 749 diseases, every 7--8B model is near the floor: $\passk{1}$ is 1.4--2.2\% and $\passk{32}$ 3.7--7.0\% for the Qwen-family models, and lower for OLMo~3 7B (2.5--4.5\%).
Stated confidence is not near the floor. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct states a mean top-1 probability of 0.51, and its RL-trained medical descendants state 0.67 (HuatuoGPT-o1) and 0.69 (AlphaMed), while their accuracy is about 2\%. ECE accordingly rises from 0.49 to 0.64--0.67; at this accuracy ECE mostly restates mean confidence, so we read it as a rise in confidence without a matching rise in accuracy.
The RL-trained medical models (AlphaMed; HuatuoGPT-o1 after SFT) thus state much higher confidence where they are least accurate. The effect also appears without instruction tuning: AlphaMed's base-initialized checkpoint is the most overconfident Qwen-family model (mean confidence 0.71, ECE 0.70, accuracy 1.7\%).
The medical descendants do gain a little accuracy: AlphaMed beats its parent at every $k$ ($+0.6$ $[+0.2,+0.9]$ points at $k{=}1$, $+1.0$ $[+0.1,+2.1]$ at $k{=}32$), HuatuoGPT-o1 at $k \le 8$, and m1 at $k \ge 2$ ($+1.4$ $[+0.4,+2.5]$ at $k{=}32$). These gains are an order of magnitude smaller than the rise in confidence.
m1, trained only with supervised reasoning traces (at its intended 8k-token budget), is the exception on calibration and diversity: it is the most diverse model (15.3 distinct answers per 32 samples, against 11.5 for the parent and 9.2 for HuatuoGPT-o1), and its confidence rises least (0.57).

General post-training reproduces the DDXPlus pattern on Qwen3. Qwen3-8B beats its base at $\passk{1}$ ($+0.7$ $[+0.4,+1.1]$) and trails it from $k{=}8$ on, by $-2.2$ $[-3.1,-1.3]$ points at $\passk{32}$ (a relative loss of 37\%), with half the distinct answers (6.4 vs.\ 13.4). For Qwen2.5 no pass@$k$ difference is significant.
Unlike medical RL, general post-training \emph{lowers} stated confidence here (0.61 to 0.46 for Qwen3, 0.61 to 0.51 for Qwen2.5), although base models also give invalid answers more often (8.5\% of Qwen3-8B-Base samples).
OLMo~3 does not share this: every stage of its pipeline states a mean confidence of 0.73--0.84 at about 1\% accuracy ($\passk{32}$ 2.5--4.5\%), and its SFT checkpoint is the most overconfident model we evaluate (confidence 0.84, ECE 0.83).
On the 500-case subset, our 8B GRPO runs (\S\ref{sec:training}) are indistinguishable from their base (all pass@$k$ differences within $\pm0.6$ points).

Accuracy depends strongly on when the source case report was published (Table~\ref{tab:era}). For every model, $\passk{32}$ is 5--10\% on cases published up to 2022 ($n{=}901$), at most 1.7\% on 2023--24 cases ($n{=}348$), and 2.6--4.6\% on 2025+ cases ($n{=}152$). The pattern is not monotone, since 2025+ cases score above 2023--24 cases, so case mix also matters; the gap between older and recent cases is consistent with, but does not establish, memorization of older case reports.

\section{GRPO with a group-level reward}\label{sec:training}
We also fine-tuned Qwen3-1.7B (instruct) and Qwen3-8B-Base with GRPO (LoRA, 200 steps, 16 samples per prompt, KL coefficient 0.04) on DDXPlus training patients disjoint from the test split, comparing the standard correctness reward (\texttt{acc}) with a group-level reward (\texttt{gm}). If $k_a$ of the $n$ samples in a GRPO group give top answer $a$, \texttt{gm} adds $\operatorname{clip}(n\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(a) - (k_a - 1), 0, 1)$, which pays an answer only while its condition is under-represented relative to its reference mass. At 1.7B, a boosted variant (weight 3, training temperature 1.2, so the two changes are confounded) raised pass@1 by 1.6 and distinct answers by 0.10 over \texttt{acc} (two seeds; intervals ignore seed variation). From Qwen3-8B-Base, both recipes reached nearly the official Qwen3-8B's top-1 accuracy (0.537 and 0.539 vs.\ 0.558) while keeping more than twice its distinct answers (2.7--2.9 vs.\ 1.27), and \texttt{gm} narrowed the base model less than \texttt{acc} (distinct answers $+0.14$ at equal pass@1). Neither changed Phenopacket accuracy. Because \texttt{gm} needs a reference differential at training time, which real clinical data rarely provide, and the runs train on the evaluation distribution, these are existence results only. Closely related rewards computed from group answer counts have appeared since: a witness advantage that trains models to sample the distributions they state~\citep{arabi2026samplewhat}, and a batch bonus that penalizes repeated answers~\citep{song2025outcome}; we do not compare against them. The GRPO runs enter only the 56-combination audit (\S\ref{sec:audit}).

\paragraph{Pooled effects and equivalence.} Statements such as ``robust only for Qwen'' compare significance across pairs.
