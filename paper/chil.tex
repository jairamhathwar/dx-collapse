\begin{abstract}
A language model's differential diagnosis can be read from the list it states in one answer or from the spread of its answers over repeated samples, the readout behind pass@$k$, self-consistency and sampling-based uncertainty. We measure how post-training changes both readouts on DDXPlus, whose synthetic patients carry a rule-based reference differential, with base/post-trained pairs from three open families at 7--32B (Qwen2.5 and Qwen3, every released stage of OLMo~3 7B and OLMo~3.1 32B, and Mistral-7B), zero- and few-shot prompts, and real rare-disease cases.
Post-training largely removes the variation between calls. Sampled answers collapse (1.3--2.4 distinct top answers per 32 samples at $T{=}0.7$, against 3.8--4.9), and once every model sees worked examples the post-trained models lose 14--30 points of pass@32; for one of two models tested, sampling at a higher temperature recovers most of this.
One stated list moves closer to the reference, but only by naming conditions with more reference mass, while its probabilities among those conditions move away, and only by about as much as averaging five base-model lists; the gain is robust for Qwen, weak for OLMo~3.1 32B and Mistral, and absent for OLMo~3 7B. Post-training also shifts which severe conditions are named, in both directions, rather than dropping them overall.
Majority voting stops helping and agreement between samples weakens as an error signal; averaging the ranks of agreement and stated probability, with no labels, recovers most of what a labeled combination gains.
Qwen3-8B's thinking mode reverses much of this: it beats its base model at pass@32 and restores agreement as the better error signal, though its answers stay narrow.
Studies of diagnostic breadth should report which readout they use and whether the model reasons first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{pg:mainstart}
Clinicians reason over a differential diagnosis, a ranked set of conditions consistent with the presentation, and the conditions they keep in view determine which tests are ordered and which dangerous possibilities are ruled out.
A language model's differential can be read in two ways: from the list it states in one answer, or from the distribution of its answers over repeated samples.
Much current practice uses the second. Pass@$k$, self-consistency voting~\citep{wang2023selfconsistency} and sampling-based uncertainty such as semantic entropy~\citep{farquhar2024semanticentropy} draw on repeated samples, and so does the evidence that reinforcement learning sharpens models, which win at $\passk{1}$ while their base models solve more problems given many samples~\citep{yue2025rlboundary,wu2025invisibleleash,oh2026sharpeningtax}.
The two readouts agree for a model that samples from the distribution it states. Instruction-tuned models do not: they verbalize distributions they fail to sample from~\citep{meister2025distributional,zhang2025verbalized,jang2026cannotsample}, and one aligned answer can cover what many base-model samples spread out~\citep{lake2025overton}. \citet{jang2026cannotsample} show this on synthetic and survey targets, including for Qwen2.5-7B and Mistral-7B, two of our pairs.
We ask what it means for differential diagnosis, where every case has a reference differential and a true diagnosis, and what it does to the tools that read the differential from samples. This is a replication and extension in a clinical setting, not a new phenomenon.

The design holds the pretrained weights fixed and varies only the post-training: base/instruct pairs for Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and Mistral-7B; every released stage (SFT, DPO, RL) of OLMo~3 7B~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} and OLMo~3.1 32B, plus a 7B checkpoint trained with RL directly from the base; three medical models fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; and Qwen3-8B with its thinking mode on. Each final base/post-trained pair is also run with worked examples in the prompt, so that base models answer in the requested format.
Our measurements:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sampled answers collapse} in every family, more so once base models are prompted with worked examples, and the stage that narrows them most depends on the recipe (\S\ref{sec:sampled}).
\item \textbf{One post-trained call does the averaging.} The stated list improves by naming better conditions, not by stating better probabilities, and by about as much as averaging five base-model lists; severe conditions are reshuffled rather than dropped (\S\ref{sec:stated}).
\item \textbf{Sampling-based methods lose value.} Voting stops helping and agreement weakens as an error signal; a label-free rank average of agreement and stated probability is a sound default (\S\ref{sec:consequences}).
\item \textbf{Reasoning reverses much of it.} Qwen3-8B's thinking mode restores pass@32 and agreement, though not breadth (\S\ref{sec:sampled},~\ref{sec:consequences}); on real rare-disease cases the direction replicates (\S\ref{sec:medrare}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Post-training and the sampled distribution.}
RL with verifiable rewards improves $\passk{1}$ while base models win at large $k$ on math and code~\citep{yue2025rlboundary}, reweighting solutions already in the base model's support~\citep{wu2025invisibleleash}; a ``sharpening tax'' appears across 14 base/post-trained pairs on agentic tasks~\citep{oh2026sharpeningtax}, though RL can expand the boundary in domains under-represented in pretraining~\citep{cheng2025guru}. In medicine, the one pass@$k$ analysis we know of studies vision-language models~\citep{jeddi2026medvlmrl}. Instruction tuning and RLHF reduce output diversity~\citep{kirk2024rlhfdiversity,west2025basemodels,jiang2025hivemind,murthy2025onefish}, more so with scale for SFT~\citep{springer2026annotations}; \citet{liu2026alignmenttax} place the collapse at DPO in a Zephyr ablation, while \citet{jang2026cannotsample} find it growing at each OLMo-2 stage.

\paragraph{Stated versus sampled distributions.}
Models asked to verbalize a distribution match it better than their samples do, on opinion surveys~\citep{meister2025distributional} and on synthetic and survey targets~\citep{jang2026cannotsample}, and they struggle to draw from target distributions directly~\citep{gu2026illusion}. Verbalized sampling keeps most of the base model's diversity through Tülu~3's SFT, DPO and RLVR checkpoints while direct sampling collapses~\citep{zhang2025verbalized}, and aligned answers aggregate content that base models spread across samples~\citep{lake2025overton}. Our stated readout is the verbalized top-$k$ elicitation of \citet{tian2023justask}. For error detection, sample consistency usually beats verbalized confidence and hybrids beat both~\citep{xiong2024express,lyu2024consistency}, including for medical diagnosis~\citep{savage2025uncertainty,ren2026medconf}, and aligned models' samples can collapse enough to leave sampling-based uncertainty without signal~\citep{liu2026alignmenttax}. We add a clinical task with a reference differential and a true diagnosis, three pipelines at 7--32B with a few-shot control, and the consequences for aggregation and uncertainty.

\paragraph{Medical models and differential diagnosis.}
LLMs have been evaluated as differential-diagnosis aids on clinicopathological cases~\citep{kanjee2023jama,mcduff2025ddx}; on Phenopacket Store, LLMs trail the phenotype-driven tool Exomiser~\citep{reese2026phenopacket}. Medical post-training recipes include verifier-guided SFT and PPO~\citep{chen2024huatuogpto1}, rule-based RL~\citep{liu2025alphamed} and reasoning distillation~\citep{huang2025m1}; RL and reward models tend to favor confident answers~\citep{leng2025taming,damani2025rlcr,mei2025reasoninguncertainty}. DDXPlus was built for diagnosis systems trained on differentials~\citep{fansi2022casande} and scores predicted against reference differentials as sets; H-DDx proposes hierarchical F1 over ICD-10~\citep{h-ddx2025}, and MEDDxAgent studies interactive diagnosis on it~\citep{rose2025meddxagent}. \citet{puri2026beyondmode} show on DDXPlus with Qwen3-8B that single-answer RL collapses sampled answers and train models to output several answers per completion. We compare stated and sampled differentials against the reference \emph{distribution} across public post-training pipelines.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Tasks.}
\textbf{DDXPlus}~\citep{fansi2022ddxplus}: synthetic patients from a rule-based medical knowledge base, each with a true pathology and a reference differential, the engine's posterior over 49 conditions. We use 980 test patients (20 per condition) rendered by template as questionnaire-style vignettes without exam or test results (example in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The prompt lists the 49 conditions and asks for the five most likely, with probabilities, as JSON, so this is a closed-set task.
\textbf{Phenopacket Store}~\citep{danis2025phenopacketstore}: 1,401 published rare-disease cases covering 749 diseases (at most two per disease), rendered as phenotype vignettes with an open-ended differential; names are graded against the case's diagnosis via Mondo as exact, related (a specific ancestor group or subtype) or wrong (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\paragraph{Models and sampling.}
Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B and Mistral-7B-v0.3, each with its official post-trained version; OLMo-3-1025-7B and OLMo-3-1125-32B with the SFT, DPO and final RLVR (Instruct) checkpoints of OLMo~3 7B and OLMo~3.1 32B, plus OLMo-3-7B-RL-Zero-General, trained with RL from the 7B base; and AlphaMed (rule-based RL), HuatuoGPT-o1 (SFT then PPO) and m1 (SFT distillation), all fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Table~\ref{tab:models}). We draw 32 samples per case with vLLM at $T{=}0.7$ (top-$p$ 0.95) and, for the 7--8B models, $T{=}1$ (top-$p$ 1), each model with its own chat template (base models without one get a post-trained sibling's) and Qwen3's thinking mode off unless stated. A sample's \emph{top answer} is valid if it is one of the 49 conditions and its \emph{list} if it names at least one; invalid answers count as wrong. With this zero-shot prompt, base models are often invalid (up to 47\% of samples for OLMo and 52\% for Mistral), so every final pair is re-run with three worked examples from the training split (Appendix~\ref{sec:fewshot}), on which every model is valid in over 99\% of samples.

\paragraph{Two readouts.}
The \emph{sampled} readout is the distribution of top answers over a case's samples: $\passk{k}$~\citep{chen2021codex}, the number of distinct top answers, the reference mass they cover (coverage@32), and total-variation distance (TV) of their empirical distribution to the reference.
The \emph{stated} readout is one sample's list, with its stated probabilities renormalized over the conditions it names (uniform if missing), averaged per case over valid samples: its TV to the reference, also after removing the true pathology from both, DDXPlus's set F1 against the reference's support (DDF1), the reference mass it names, and whether it names each severe condition (DDXPlus severity 1--2, the dataset's label) that the reference weights at least 0.1. A list $q$ with named set $L$ has $\mathrm{TV} = (1-\sum_{c\in L} p_c) + \sum_{c \in L}\max(0, p_c - q_c)$ against reference $p$: the mass it leaves out plus the mass it \emph{misallocates} among the conditions it names.
The \emph{single-call answer} is the top answer of a model's first valid sample.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
DDXPlus has 20 cases per true pathology, so intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from a bootstrap that resamples the 49 pathologies with all their cases (10,000 resamples) unless stated; p-values use the add-one correction, and the headline base-vs-post differences are Holm-corrected over 112 tests (Appendix~\ref{app:clustered}). Appendix intervals are from a paired case bootstrap, two to three times narrower, unless marked as clustered. AUROCs use average ranks for ties. With five to six pipelines, results describe these checkpoints rather than post-training in general.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Post-training collapses the sampled answers}\label{sec:sampled}
Table~\ref{tab:sampled} compares each base model with its post-trained versions.
At 7--8B the post-trained Qwen model is far better on its first answer ($+12.6$ to $+27.5$ points), but its pass@$k$ curve crosses the base model's at $k{\approx}4$--8, and at $k{=}32$ the base model leads by 9.7--14.6 points (Figure~\ref{fig:passk} in Appendix~\ref{sec:general}). At 32B the zero-shot losses are smaller and uncertain (Qwen2.5-32B $-4.9$ $[-10.2,+0.6]$, OLMo~3.1 32B $-4.0$ $[-6.8,-1.2]$, neither surviving correction). With worked examples in every prompt, base models answer validly and post-training costs 14--30 points of pass@32 in all six final pairs, including both 32B pairs and Mistral-7B (Appendices~\ref{sec:fewshot} and~\ref{sec:mistral}), also with example lists taken from another model instead of the reference (Appendix~\ref{sec:controls}). Part of the narrowing is sharper sampling that a higher temperature can undo for some models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ matches its base model's pass@32 at $T{=}0.7$ (0.704 against 0.695) at a cost of 4.2 points of pass@1, whereas Qwen3-8B stays below its base model even at $T{=}1.6$ (0.684 against 0.732).
Post-trained models give 1.3--2.4 distinct top answers per 32 samples at $T{=}0.7$, against 3.8--4.9 for their base models. Coverage@32 rewards spreading answers: a guesser drawing uniformly from the 49 conditions covers 0.48 and one drawing from the average reference differential 0.56, more than any model. TV does not reward spreading for its own sake: a sampler that always gives each case's reference mode scores 0.694, and one that always gives the true pathology 0.714, both better than the patient-blind guesser (0.774; Table~\ref{tab:stated}). Base models' samples lie in between (0.73--0.76 at $T{=}0.7$), and post-trained samples are farther than either (0.76--0.85): they are concentrated, and often on the wrong condition.
Which stage narrows the samples most depends on the recipe. In OLMo~3 7B, distinct answers fall from 4.93 to 4.01 after SFT, 3.30 after DPO and 2.37 after RLVR, and the RLVR step alone costs 19.1 $[13.8,24.6]$ points of $\passk{32}$ against the DPO model; in OLMo~3.1 32B, SFT costs most (12.1 $[6.8,18.0]$), DPO recovers most of it, and RLVR costs 2.8 $[0.5,5.3]$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:big32}). RL-Zero, trained with RL from the 7B base, loses 13.3 $[7.3,19.7]$ points at $T{=}0.7$, about as much as SFT, and none detectably at $T{=}1$ ($-1.8$ $[-5.8,+2.1]$; Appendix~\ref{sec:stages}).
Qwen3-8B with its thinking mode on is the exception: the same weights beat the base model at $\passk{32}$ (0.815 against 0.732; $+8.4$ $[1.7,15.3]$, not surviving correction) with samples as close to the reference as the base model's (TV 0.745 against 0.750), though they stay narrow (1.76 distinct answers; Appendix~\ref{sec:thinking}).
Severe conditions also become rarer among sampled top answers, but even base models consider them less often than the patient-blind guesser (Appendix~\ref{sec:collapse}), so we assess them on the stated list.

\subsection{One post-trained call does the averaging}\label{sec:stated}
For every Qwen model and OLMo~3.1 32B, the post-trained model's single list is closer to the reference than its base model's (TV $-0.043$ to $-0.088$; Table~\ref{tab:stated}, Figure~\ref{fig:stated}a), also with the true pathology removed from both ($-0.018$ to $-0.056$; Qwen3-8B at $T{=}0.7$ does not survive correction) and by DDXPlus's set F1 ($+0.040$ to $+0.083$). OLMo~3 7B's Instruct lists are not closer, and without the true pathology they are farther ($+0.034$ at $T{=}0.7$). With worked examples in every prompt the Qwen gains persist or grow, OLMo~3.1 32B keeps only a higher reference mass, and Mistral-7B's gain is the smallest (TV $-0.036$; Appendices~\ref{sec:fewshot},~\ref{sec:mistral}).

Two analyses show what this gain is. First, it lies entirely in the choice of conditions. The reference mass a list leaves out falls, while the mass it misallocates among the conditions it names rises after post-training in seven of eight settings ($+0.011$ to $+0.033$; Table~\ref{tab:stated}) and in every few-shot pair. Second, averaging calls closes most of the gap: the mean of five base-model lists is about as close to the reference as one post-trained list (Qwen3-8B 0.699 against 0.700, Qwen2.5-7B 0.717 against 0.717, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.691 against 0.701; diamonds in Figure~\ref{fig:stated}a). With five lists on both sides, post-training is still closer for Qwen2.5 ($-0.024$ to $-0.035$) but not detectably for Qwen3-8B, OLMo~3.1 32B or Mistral, and farther for OLMo~3 7B (Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregate}). Direct-answer post-training thus mostly packs into one call what the base model spreads across calls, as \citet{lake2025overton} find for opinions. Qwen3-8B's thinking mode goes further: one of its lists (TV 0.670) beats five averaged base lists, and five of its lists beat five base lists by 0.049.

\paragraph{Severe conditions.}
Post-trained lists name the severe conditions that the reference weights at least 0.1 more often in every setting (Qwen $+9.0$ to $+13.8$ points, OLMo~3 7B $+6.0$ and $+8.7$; OLMo~3.1 32B $+2.2$, n.s.), yet one list still names only 0.32--0.47 of them, against 0.975 for the reference's own top five. Condition by condition (any reference weight; Table~\ref{tab:severetest}), post-training reshuffles rather than drops: after false-discovery-rate correction, 2--7 of the 17 severe conditions are named significantly less often and 0--6 significantly more often, and the number named less often is never larger than chance would give. The shifts have a pattern. The Qwen models name acute coronary syndromes and pulmonary embolism more often and stable angina or acute pulmonary edema less often, OLMo~3 7B names pulmonary embolism, epiglottitis and myocarditis more often and the coronary syndromes less often, and OLMo~3.1 32B names acute pulmonary edema and NSTEMI less often with no significant gains. These vignettes lack the ECG and biomarkers that separate the coronary syndromes, so the shifts track the reference engine, not clinical correctness.

\subsection{Consequences for sampling-based methods}\label{sec:consequences}
\paragraph{Aggregation.}
Majority voting over 32 samples (self-consistency) raises base models' accuracy by 3.4--8.4 points at $T{=}0.7$ but adds at most 1.9 for the final post-trained models (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, $[0.8,3.1]$; the others n.s.; Table~\ref{tab:consequences}). Models that reason before answering still gain (HuatuoGPT-o1 $+4.8$, m1 $+3.8$, Qwen3-8B with a CoT prompt $+4.5$), and Qwen3-8B with thinking on gains $+1.5$ (n.s.). For a top-three differential, the top three of one list contains the true pathology more often than the three most frequent of 32 sampled answers for the post-trained Qwen models ($+5.6$ to $+13.7$ points), while for every base model the samples win by 7.3--13.6.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
We score each signal by how well it ranks the correctness of the single-call answer (AUROC). The stated probability needs no extra calls; \emph{agreement}, the share of other samples with the same top answer, needs 4 or 31. For base models, agreement across 31 samples beats the stated probability by 0.07--0.26. Post-training weakens agreement in every family (Qwen3-8B 0.810 $\rightarrow$ 0.655, Qwen2.5-7B 0.811 $\rightarrow$ 0.702, Qwen2.5-32B 0.797 $\rightarrow$ 0.721, OLMo~3 7B 0.802 $\rightarrow$ 0.731, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.831 $\rightarrow$ 0.772; Figure~\ref{fig:stated}b), significantly after correction for Qwen but not for OLMo, consistent with \citet{liu2026alignmenttax}. The stated probability then wins for Qwen3-8B ($+0.079$ $[0.011,0.143]$ over 32-sample agreement) and, against five-sample agreement, for OLMo-3-7B-Instruct ($+0.128$ $[0.052,0.203]$); for the other final models agreement stays better or ties, in line with \citet{savage2025uncertainty}. Thinking returns Qwen3-8B to the base pattern (agreement 0.732 against 0.638), and so does sampling Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ (0.794 against 0.602; Appendix~\ref{sec:controls}).

\paragraph{Which signal to use.}
Across 56 model--setting combinations (descriptive, since they share weights; Appendix~\ref{sec:audit}), the stated probability's advantage over five-sample agreement tracks how often five samples all agree (Spearman 0.75), partly by construction, since agreement is then tied. The practical default needs no labels: averaging the two signals' ranks gives a held-out AUROC of 0.746 for the 39 post-trained combinations, against 0.695 for always using agreement and 0.671 for always using the stated probability. A logistic combination fitted on 100 labeled cases adds little (0.753), and picking the better single signal on those cases does worse (0.720).

\paragraph{Ambiguity.}
For every model and signal, the Spearman correlation between uncertainty and the entropy of the case's reference differential lies between $-0.11$ and $+0.16$, except AlphaMed's stated probability ($+0.30$): by either readout, a model's uncertainty says almost nothing about how ambiguous a case is.

\subsection{Real rare-disease cases and medical post-training}\label{sec:medrare}
\paragraph{Real rare-disease cases.} Phenopacket Store has no reference differential, and 7--8B models are near the floor ($\passk{32}$ 2.5--7.0\%), so we count an answer as correct if it is the diagnosis or a closely related Mondo class (36--130 correct single-call answers per model; Appendix~\ref{sec:phenoreadouts}) and read these results as directional. Majority voting helps the base models by 3.1--4.6 points but the final post-trained models by only 0.4--1.5. Post-training weakens agreement as an error signal (Qwen3-8B 0.830 $\rightarrow$ 0.763, Qwen2.5-7B 0.845 $\rightarrow$ 0.745, OLMo~3 7B 0.814 $\rightarrow$ 0.726) and strengthens the stated probability (0.567 $\rightarrow$ 0.707, 0.598 $\rightarrow$ 0.717, 0.464 $\rightarrow$ 0.652), but unlike on DDXPlus agreement stays the better signal (by 0.03--0.07 after post-training, detectably only for Qwen3-8B), and the three most frequent sampled answers still contain a correct diagnosis more often than one list's top three (Qwen3-8B: a tie).

\paragraph{Medical models.} Medical post-training on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct does not narrow the samples further: AlphaMed (rule-based RL) improves on its parent at large $k$ ($+8.4$ $[2.6,14.7]$ points at $k{=}32$) with unchanged diversity (1.87 vs.\ 1.82 distinct answers), and HuatuoGPT-o1 and m1, both trained on reasoning traces, widen the sampled differential (2.49 and 3.29; Appendix~\ref{sec:medical}).
On Phenopacket Store, stated confidence is not near the floor: the RL-trained medical models (AlphaMed; HuatuoGPT-o1 after SFT) raise it from 0.51 for the parent to 0.67--0.69 while accuracy gains stay under 1.5 points, and every OLMo~3 7B stage states 0.73--0.84 at about 1\% accuracy (Appendix~\ref{sec:rare}).

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\citet{jang2026cannotsample} show that instruction-tuned models cannot sample from distributions they can describe. In differential diagnosis, against a reference with a known true pathology, we find the same split and a simpler account of it: direct-answer post-training largely removes the variation between calls. The sampled answers collapse, and the single stated list improves mainly because one call now returns about what several base-model calls give on average, by naming better conditions rather than by stating better probabilities. Evaluations that read the differential from samples (pass@$k$ at large $k$, diversity, self-consistency, agreement-based uncertainty) therefore report a loss that a clinician reading one answer would not see, and these tools lose value on post-trained models; a label-free rank average of agreement and stated probability is a reasonable default error signal. Conversely, a better stated list is no evidence that post-training adds diagnostic knowledge: averaged base lists match it, every model stays far from the reference, neither readout tracks how ambiguous a case is, and the reshuffling of severe conditions is something a must-not-miss review would need to check model by model. These effects belong to answering directly at the usual temperatures: Qwen3-8B's thinking mode restores large-$k$ accuracy and agreement as an error signal, though not breadth, and hotter sampling does much the same for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct but not for Qwen3-8B. Which stage narrows the samples most is a property of the recipe.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
DDXPlus is synthetic and closed-set: its reference differential is a rule-based engine's posterior over questionnaire answers without exam or test results, its test set is class-balanced, and the prompt lists all 49 conditions, so TV, coverage and severe-condition naming measure agreement with that engine, not clinical correctness, and no clinician reviewed the lists or the references. Severity is the dataset's label, and the prompt asks for the most likely conditions, not for must-not-miss ones. The few-shot controls use one temperature and one set of example patients, with lists from the reference or from Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, and the temperature control covers two 7--8B models. We study open 7--32B models from three families, one reasoning mode, one RL-Zero checkpoint and no frontier models, and the 32B models only at $T{=}0.7$. The signal combinations are validated only within DDXPlus. Phenopacket accuracy is near the floor, its grader is rule-based and not validated against clinicians, and its cases may be in pretraining data.

\section{Prompts, vignettes and grading}\label{app:prompts}
\sloppy
\paragraph{DDXPlus.} Each patient's coded evidence is rendered by a fixed template into a vignette of the patient's answers and relevant history; we fix known mistranslations in the English evidence descriptions. An example (test case with true pathology chronic rhinosinusitis; the reference differential gives viral pharyngitis 0.17, chronic rhinosinusitis 0.16, cluster headache 0.15, acute rhinosinusitis 0.14, bronchitis 0.14, URTI 0.12):
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
A 13-year-old female presents for evaluation. Symptoms (patient answers): Do you have pain somewhere, related to your reason for consulting? Yes. Characterize your pain: burning. Do you feel pain somewhere? mouth, forehead, cheek(R), cheek(L), nose. How intense is the pain? (scale 0-10) 3. [\dots] Have you lost your sense of smell? Yes. Do you have nasal congestion or a clear runny nose? Yes. Do you have a cough? Yes. Relevant history and risk factors: Have you had a cold in the last 2 weeks? Yes. [\dots] Do you have polyps in your nose? Yes. [\dots]
\end{quote}
System prompt: \texttt{You are an experienced emergency and primary care physician.} The user turn is the vignette followed by: \texttt{Based on the patient's answers above, give your differential diagnosis. Choose ONLY from this list of possible conditions: [the 49 conditions, one per line]. List the 5 most likely conditions from the list, most likely first, with your probability that each is the correct diagnosis. Use the condition names exactly as written in the list. End your answer with a JSON array in exactly this format: [\{"diagnosis": "<name>", "probability": <number between 0 and 1>\}, ...]}. The full-distribution prompt (\S\ref{sec:fullprompt}) instead asks for a probability on every condition, summing to one, most likely first. The plain and CoT variants are described in \S\ref{sec:robust}. Base models receive the same chat-formatted prompt; OLMo base models use their SFT checkpoint's chat template.
\paragraph{Phenopacket Store.} The vignette lists the case's present and (up to ten) excluded HPO phenotypes with sex and age. System prompt: \texttt{You are an expert clinical geneticist and diagnostician.} Instructions: \texttt{List the 5 most likely specific diagnoses (use the specific disease or subtype name, e.g.\ 'Spastic paraplegia 7' rather than 'hereditary spastic paraplegia', when you can), most likely first, with your probability that each is the correct diagnosis}, followed by the same JSON format.
\paragraph{Grading open-ended names.} Each predicted name is mapped to Mondo by exact lookup against Mondo labels and synonyms (also trying any parenthesized abbreviation and the name without it), then by fuzzy matching (token-sort ratio $\geq 92$). It is \emph{exact} if it maps to the Mondo class of the case's OMIM diagnosis, and \emph{related} if it maps to a specific ancestor group of it (at most two is-a steps up, with at most 150 descendants) or to one of its subtypes; siblings sharing only a broad parent are wrong. The grader passes hand-written checks (e.g.\ ``Hereditary spastic paraplegia type 7 (SPG7)'' is exact for SPG7, ``Hereditary spastic paraplegia'' is related, and Turner syndrome is wrong for Jacobsen syndrome), but we did not validate it against clinician grading, so errors at the scale of the 1--2 point effects we report on this dataset are possible.

\subsection{The sampled differential collapses, while top-1 calibration improves}\label{sec:collapse}
The gap at large $k$ reflects a collapsed \emph{sampled} differential.
Across 32 samples, the post-trained Qwen models name 1.3--2.6 distinct diagnoses per case, against 3.8--8.2 for their base models.
Their answer entropy is 0.11--0.49, against 0.78--1.58 for base models and 1.87 for the reference differential (Table~\ref{tab:pairs}).
Sampling at $T{=}1$ widens the base models' answers but barely changes the post-trained models' (Qwen3 entropy 0.11 at $T{=}0.7$, 0.18 at $T{=}1$), so the collapse lives in the model's own distribution rather than in the decoding settings.
As a result, post-trained models cover less of the reference differential: 0.24 vs.\ 0.41 of its probability mass at $k{=}32$ for Qwen3, and 0.29 vs.\ 0.42 for Qwen2.5.
Their top-1 calibration, however, is markedly better (ECE 0.06 vs.\ 0.21 and 0.14 vs.\ 0.30).
On every single-answer criterion (accuracy, small-$k$ pass@$k$, calibration), the post-trained model therefore looks strictly better, and the loss of breadth in its samples is invisible. Whether the breadth is lost from the model's stated differential as well is a separate question (\S\ref{sec:stated}).
\paragraph{Which diagnoses drop out?}
For each case and each condition that the reference differential weights at least 0.1, we ask whether the model names that condition as its top answer in at least one of its 32 samples. We call this \emph{considering} the condition.
We group the 49 conditions by DDXPlus severity (1 = most severe).
Post-training lowers consideration in every tier (Table~\ref{tab:severity}), and the severe tier (DDXPlus severity 1--2, the dataset's label rather than a clinical consensus list) is not spared. This metric needs a chance reference, though: a guesser that picks uniformly among the 49 conditions scores about 0.48 on it, and one that draws from the average reference differential 0.56--0.61, above every model, base models included. Considered@32 therefore measures the breadth of the sampled answers, not clinical judgement; we assess can't-miss conditions on the stated list instead (\S\ref{sec:statedchecks}).
For the 17 severity-1--2 conditions (e.g.\ NSTEMI/STEMI, acute pulmonary edema, anaphylaxis, pulmonary embolism, unstable angina), consideration falls from 0.45 to 0.27 for Qwen3 and from 0.51 to 0.34 for Qwen2.5 at $T{=}1$. The differences are $-18.4$ $[-20.8,-15.9]$ and $-17.0$ $[-19.7,-14.2]$ points.
Some diagnoses disappear from the top answer almost entirely.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct names unstable angina as its top answer in one of 107 eligible cases, where its base model does in 47\%. When unstable angina is the true pathology, $\passk{32}$ falls from 1.00 to 0.00. Stable angina (0.89 to 0.06) and myocarditis (0.41 to 0.00) follow the same pattern.
These conditions are not forgotten: they still appear lower in the listed differential (unstable angina in 50\% of eligible cases, against 95\% for the base model). The model ranks them but no longer commits to them.
At the same time, post-trained models concentrate on a few salient ``attractor'' diagnoses.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct gives NSTEMI/STEMI as its top answer in 13.1\% of samples, against a reference rate of 4.4\% and 6.4\% for its base model. It also over-predicts pulmonary embolism (8.2\% vs.\ 3.2\%) and pneumonia (8.0\% vs.\ 2.4\%).
Chest-pain presentations thus collapse onto the most dangerous candidate. That raises its accuracy (NSTEMI $\passk{1}$ 0.99 vs.\ 0.44) at the cost of the alternatives, a bias toward the most dangerous candidate rather than a differential.

\subsection{Robustness to prompt and output format}\label{sec:robust}
\paragraph{Prompt.} We repeat the Qwen3-8B pair with two other prompts (Table~\ref{tab:prompts}). \emph{Plain} drops the system prompt and rewords the instructions. \emph{CoT} asks the model to reason step by step before answering; Qwen3's thinking mode stays off in all runs.
The plain prompt reproduces the pattern: the post-trained model gains $+23.0$ $[+20.9,+25.1]$ points at $k{=}1$ and loses $-7.1$ $[-9.3,-5.0]$ at $k{=}32$ ($T{=}0.7$; at $T{=}1$, $+34.2$ and $-8.7$ $[-11.0,-6.4]$), with 1.3--1.5 distinct answers against 3.7--5.6.
Asking for reasoning changes the picture. With CoT, the post-trained model's $\passk{32}$ rises from 0.607 to 0.835: it leads the base model at every $k$ at $T{=}0.7$ ($+4.7$ $[+2.9,+6.6]$ at $k{=}32$) and ties it at $T{=}1$ ($0.0$ $[-2.1,+1.9]$).
Its samples remain far less diverse than the base model's (2.1--2.2 vs.\ 3.9--6.3 distinct answers), so reasoning recovers coverage at $k{=}32$ without restoring sampling diversity.
The coverage loss is therefore largest when the model answers directly, as in most clinical decision-support deployments, and reasoning at inference time mitigates it. This matches the medical models: the reasoning-trained descendants widen the differential (\S\ref{sec:medical}).

\paragraph{Output format.} Base models more often give invalid answers: unparsable, or with a top answer outside the 49 conditions. This affects 12\% of Qwen3-8B-Base samples at $T{=}0.7$ and 25\% at $T{=}1$ (Qwen2.5-7B: 1\% and 21\%), against at most 0.3\% for the post-trained models.
Invalid samples count as wrong, which deflates the base model at large $k$ but inflates the post-trained model's gain at small $k$.
Among valid samples only, the post-trained models still gain $+12.0$ $[+9.8,+14.1]$ (Qwen3, $T{=}0.7$), $+18.7$ $[+16.6,+20.7]$ (Qwen3, $T{=}1$), $+12.2$ $[+10.4,+14.0]$ and $+16.8$ $[+15.0,+18.5]$ points (Qwen2.5) at $k{=}1$, so a quarter to a third of the raw gain at $T{=}1$ is format.
On the cases where every sample of both models is valid, the large-$k$ loss persists: $-15.2$ $[-21.9,-8.6]$ at $k{=}32$ for Qwen3 (105 cases) and $-10.2$ $[-12.7,-7.6]$ for Qwen2.5 (748 cases), both at $T{=}0.7$. (At $T{=}1$ almost no case has 32 valid base samples.) The reported large-$k$ losses are thus lower bounds.

\subsection{Which post-training stage narrows the differential?}\label{sec:stages}
The Qwen pairs compare a base model with the end of a post-training pipeline. OLMo~3~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} releases every stage of its 7B instruct pipeline from one base model: supervised fine-tuning, then DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards on math, code, instruction-following and chat prompts (the released OLMo-3-7B-Instruct). It also releases RL-Zero checkpoints trained with RL directly from the base; we use the general one, trained on chat prompts with an LM-judge reward. (The RL-Zero-Mix checkpoint does not load in our vLLM version.) Table~\ref{tab:stages} gives the results.

The coverage loss replicates in OLMo. At $T{=}0.7$, every post-trained stage is worse than the base at $k{=}32$: by $-14.6$ $[-17.5,-11.7]$ points after SFT, $-9.2$ $[-12.1,-6.1]$ after DPO, $-28.3$ $[-31.5,-25.0]$ after RLVR, and $-13.3$ $[-16.1,-10.5]$ for RL-Zero. Diversity falls at every stage of the pipeline, from 4.93 distinct answers for the base to 4.01 after SFT, 3.30 after DPO and 2.37 after RLVR.

Compared stage to stage, DPO recovers part of what SFT lost at large $k$ ($+5.4$ $[+2.9,+8.0]$ points at $k{=}32$, and $+10.3$ $[+9.0,+11.6]$ at $k{=}1$) while narrowing the differential further ($-0.71$ distinct answers).
The final RLVR stage is the largest single narrowing. Against the DPO model it loses $-19.1$ $[-21.8,-16.3]$ points at $k{=}32$, 0.92 distinct answers and 7.7 points of coverage@32, and it is also worse at $k{=}1$ ($-5.9$ $[-7.2,-4.6]$). It drops severe-tier Considered@32 to 0.25, a loss of $-16.1$ $[-18.6,-13.5]$ points relative to the base, against $-7.4$ for the DPO model. At $T{=}1$ the pattern is the same (RLVR vs.\ DPO: $-12.7$ $[-15.4,-9.9]$ at $k{=}32$).

RL applied directly to the base narrows the differential less. At $T{=}1$, RL-Zero shows no detectable loss of the base model's $\passk{32}$ ($-1.8$ $[-4.5,+0.8]$; clustered $[-5.8,+2.1]$) and 6.28 of its 7.53 distinct answers, and it loses 4.0 $[1.2,6.9]$ points of severe-tier consideration, against 14.4 for the full instruct pipeline. At $T{=}0.7$ it still loses 13.3 points at $k{=}32$ (clustered $[7.3,19.7]$), about as much as SFT.

The OLMo base model often breaks the output format: 40\% of its samples are invalid at $T{=}0.7$ and 47\% at $T{=}1$, while unparsable completions are 16--21\% for the SFT model and at most 1.6\% for the later stages. No case has 32 valid base samples, so the all-valid check of \S\ref{sec:robust} is not possible here. Among valid samples, the post-trained models' gains at $k{=}1$ are small ($-1.9$ to $+4.0$ points at $T{=}0.7$, $+1.3$ to $+8.1$ at $T{=}1$), so most of OLMo's raw gain at $k{=}1$ is format. Invalid samples count as misses and are excluded from distinct answers and entropy, so the base model's coverage and diversity are understated and the losses above are conservative.

Across these pipelines, then, the collapse of the sampled differential is not a necessary effect of RL. It accumulates over the instruction-tuning pipeline; in OLMo~3 7B its largest step is RL applied on top of SFT and DPO, but at 32B it is SFT (\S\ref{sec:big32}), so which stage narrows most depends on the recipe. RL applied directly to a base model (OLMo RL-Zero at $T{=}1$, AlphaMed's base-initialized checkpoint, and our GRPO runs in \S\ref{sec:training}) narrows the differential less, but not always: RL-Zero at $T{=}0.7$ loses about as much as SFT.
On Phenopacket Store the instruct pipeline also loses coverage: every stage trails the base model at $k{=}32$ ($-2.0$ $[-3.0,-1.1]$ points after SFT, $-1.5$ after DPO, $-1.4$ after RLVR; all significant), while RL-Zero does not ($-0.6$ $[-1.4,+0.2]$), from a base model at 4.5\% (\S\ref{sec:rare}).

\subsection{Replication at 32B}\label{sec:big32}
We repeat the DDXPlus comparisons at $T{=}0.7$ with Qwen2.5-32B and its Instruct model, and with every released stage of OLMo~3.1 32B, whose base is OLMo-3-1125-32B; there is no 32B RL-Zero checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:big32}).
The sampled differential collapses at 32B too, by about half as much. Post-training costs 4.9 $[2.8,7.1]$ points at $\passk{32}$ for Qwen2.5-32B and 4.0 $[2.0,5.9]$ for OLMo~3.1 32B (clustered $[-0.6,10.2]$ and $[1.2,6.8]$; neither survives correction), halves the number of distinct answers (3.78 $\rightarrow$ 1.68 and 4.32 $\rightarrow$ 2.15), and lowers severe-tier consideration by 6.1 and 8.4 points. For Qwen the loss holds on the 421 cases where every base sample is valid ($-4.8$ $[-7.8,-1.7]$); the OLMo 32B base gives invalid answers on 27\% of samples. With few-shot prompts, which nearly remove invalid answers, both losses are three to five times larger (\S\ref{sec:fewshot}).
The stage attribution differs from 7B. In OLMo~3.1 32B the largest narrowing is SFT ($\passk{32}$ $-12.1$ $[-14.4,-9.9]$, clustered $[-18.0,-6.8]$, distinct answers $-1.39$, severe tier $-11.9$); DPO recovers most of it ($+10.9$ against SFT), and the final RL step is small ($-2.8$ $[-4.3,-1.1]$ at $k{=}32$, clustered $[-5.3,-0.5]$, $-0.14$ distinct answers). OLMo~3.1 32B and OLMo~3 7B were post-trained with different recipes, so the stage that narrows the samples most is a property of the pipeline, not of SFT, DPO or RL as such.
Under this prompt the stated readout improves in both 32B families (\S\ref{sec:stated}; with few-shot prompts only Qwen2.5-32B's does robustly, \S\ref{sec:fewshot}), and voting stops paying off: majority voting over 32 samples gains the base models 7.0 and 8.4 points and the Instruct models 1.9 and 0.9 (the latter n.s.).

\subsection{Ruling out format, accuracy and floor effects}\label{sec:statedchecks}
Table~\ref{tab:robust} tests simpler explanations of the stated-list result.
\emph{Format.} The general models' post-trained lists and base-model lists at $T{=}0.7$ are nearly all complete (81--99\% name five on-list conditions with probabilities; Qwen base models at $T{=}1$, 61--62\%; the medical models, 61--80\%). Restricting to complete lists leaves the gaps unchanged (Qwen2.5-7B: 0.779 vs.\ 0.719).
\emph{Accuracy.} A more accurate model puts more stated mass on the true pathology, which the reference also favors. Within samples whose top answer is correct, and within those whose top answer is wrong, the post-trained Qwen list is still closer to the reference ($-0.018$ to $-0.034$ and $-0.036$ to $-0.079$ in TV across both temperatures; Table~\ref{tab:robust} shows $T{=}0.7$).
\emph{Floor.} One list can do no better than the reference's own top five, and a finite set of samples no better than as many draws from the reference. Measured as excess over these floors, post-training lowers the list's TV (Qwen3-8B 0.479 $\rightarrow$ 0.435) and raises the samples' (0.557 $\rightarrow$ 0.613).
\emph{True pathology removed.} With the true pathology removed from both list and reference and both renormalized, the post-trained Qwen and OLMo~3.1 32B lists are still closer to the reference ($-0.018$ to $-0.056$), and DDXPlus's set F1 against the reference's support improves by 0.040--0.083 (Table~\ref{tab:clustered}). TV, mass and DDF1 are not independent: a list's TV is the mass it leaves out plus the mass it misallocates among the conditions it names (Table~\ref{tab:stated}), and the misallocation rises after post-training in seven of eight settings, so the TV gain comes from the named set.
\emph{Patient-blind list.} A fixed list of the five conditions with the highest average reference probability scores TV 0.794, mass 0.238 and DDF1 0.272. Every model's list beats it in TV except OLMo~3 7B's; in DDF1 several base lists do not (Qwen2.5-7B 0.245, OLMo~3 7B 0.225), and the post-trained Qwen and OLMo~3.1 32B lists exceed it by 0.035--0.061.
\emph{Over-naming.} As a share of named conditions, post-trained lists name fewer that the reference gives less than 1\% (Qwen3-8B 0.59 $\rightarrow$ 0.55) and fewer unwarranted severe conditions (Qwen2.5-32B 0.74 $\rightarrow$ 0.65), but as a count per list the change is mixed: from 0.22 fewer unwarranted severe conditions (OLMo~3.1 32B) to 0.35 more (OLMo~3 7B).
\emph{Severe conditions one by one.} Table~\ref{tab:severetest} tests each of the 17 severe conditions, counting it wherever the reference gives it any weight. After Benjamini--Hochberg correction, 2--7 conditions per pair are named significantly less often after post-training and 0--6 significantly more often, and no pair names more conditions less often than a random swap of base and post-trained lists would. Significant losses include acute pulmonary edema (Qwen3-8B 0.75 $\rightarrow$ 0.61, Qwen2.5-32B 0.63 $\rightarrow$ 0.44, OLMo~3 7B 0.56 $\rightarrow$ 0.31, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.57 $\rightarrow$ 0.41), stable angina (Qwen2.5-7B 0.25 $\rightarrow$ 0.08), NSTEMI/STEMI (OLMo~3 7B 0.31 $\rightarrow$ 0.14, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.49 $\rightarrow$ 0.36) and myocarditis (Qwen2.5-32B 0.14 $\rightarrow$ 0.05); significant gains include pulmonary embolism (Qwen2.5-7B 0.29 $\rightarrow$ 0.56, OLMo~3 7B 0.20 $\rightarrow$ 0.65), NSTEMI/STEMI (Qwen2.5-7B 0.32 $\rightarrow$ 0.52) and unstable angina (Qwen3-8B 0.14 $\rightarrow$ 0.37). Among single stages, OLMo~3 7B's RLVR step and OLMo~3.1 32B's SFT and RLVR steps do name more conditions less often than chance (12--13 of 17, $p{=}0.03$--0.04).

\subsection{Few-shot elicitation}\label{sec:fewshot}
The main analyses give every model the same zero-shot chat prompt, which base models were not trained to follow; they give invalid answers on up to 47\% of samples, which we count as wrong. We therefore re-ran the five final base/post-trained pairs at $T{=}0.7$ with three worked examples in the prompt: three fixed DDXPlus training patients with different true pathologies, each followed by the top five conditions of its reference differential, with probabilities, in the JSON format the instructions ask for. Base models see the instructions and examples as a plain document and complete the next one (generation stops at the end of the list); post-trained models see the same instructions and examples as earlier chat turns. Invalid samples fall below 0.5\% for every model (Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}).

The examples help base models most. Their pass@1 rises from 0.178 to 0.306 for OLMo-3-1025-7B, from 0.326 to 0.528 for OLMo-3-1125-32B and from 0.500 to 0.647 for Qwen2.5-32B, and their pass@32 reaches 0.76--0.90, while their stated lists barely change (TV 0.712--0.787, against 0.726--0.794 zero-shot). The losses in the samples therefore grow (Table~\ref{tab:fewshotdiff}): post-training costs 15.2--29.9 points of pass@32 in every pair, including both 32B pairs, whose zero-shot losses did not survive correction, and every post-trained model's Cov@32 (0.21--0.25) is below every base model's (0.33--0.45) and below a uniform guesser's.

For the three Qwen pairs the stated-list improvement persists and mostly grows: TV $-0.057$ to $-0.087$, $-0.055$ to $-0.073$ with the true pathology removed, and DDF1 $+0.051$ to $+0.081$, all surviving correction. OLMo~3 7B remains the exception (TV $+0.021$, n.s.; $+0.024$ without the true pathology). For OLMo~3.1 32B the improvement shrinks: the post-trained list still places more reference mass on the conditions it names ($+0.047$), but its TV ($-0.022$), TV without the true pathology ($-0.006$) and DDF1 ($+0.009$) no longer survive correction. At 32B the stated-list result is therefore robust for Qwen2.5-32B but depends on the prompt for OLMo~3.1 32B. Post-trained lists still name 5--10 of the 17 severe conditions less often than their base model's.

The consequences replicate in all five pairs. Majority voting over 32 samples gains base models 6.2--10.9 points and post-trained models 0.2--1.1. The three most frequent of 32 sampled answers beat the top three of one stated list by 8.2--22.0 points for base models, while for post-trained models the list is ahead by 0.2--16.7. Agreement with four more samples is the better error signal for every base model (AUROC 0.79--0.84, against 0.67--0.73 for the stated probability), and the stated probability for every post-trained model (0.69--0.77, against 0.56--0.68).
This check uses one temperature and one set of examples, and the examples show the reference's own lists, which may help every model match the reference.

\subsection{Two further controls}\label{sec:controls}
\paragraph{Reference-free few-shot examples.} The examples of \S\ref{sec:fewshot} show each training patient's reference top five, and so also the target of the evaluation. We re-ran the six final pairs with the same three patients but with each example list replaced by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct's own greedy answer (on-list, with the true pathology first for two patients and second for one). The conclusions do not change (Table~\ref{tab:refree}). Post-training costs 12.7--29.7 points of pass@32 in every pair. The stated list moves closer to the reference for the three Qwen pairs and, now clearly, for Mistral (TV $-0.053$ to $-0.077$; $-0.031$ to $-0.059$ with the true pathology removed), but not for OLMo~3 7B or OLMo~3.1 32B, and the misallocation among named conditions rises wherever the list improves. Voting helps base models by 3.7--13.7 points and post-trained ones by at most 1.4; the top three of one list beats the three most frequent samples for every post-trained model; the stated probability beats agreement as an error signal for every post-trained model and agreement beats it for every base model; and no pair names more severe conditions less often than chance. Base models score lower at $k{=}1$ than with reference-derived examples (OLMo-3-1125-32B 0.453 against 0.528).

\paragraph{Matched temperature.} Post-trained models are sharper than their base models at the same nominal temperature, so part of the narrowing could be colder effective sampling. We sampled Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $T{=}1.3$ and 1.6 (Table~\ref{tab:matcht}), and the two models differ. Qwen3-8B barely broadens: at $T{=}1.6$ it gives 1.92 distinct answers and reaches 0.684 at pass@32, still below its base model at $T{=}0.7$ (0.732) and at $T{=}1$ (0.789; $-10.5$ points, surviving correction); voting gains stay under 2 points, agreement stays weaker than for the base model, and its stated list does not change (TV 0.701). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct does broaden: at $T{=}1.3$, with 2\% invalid answers, it gives 3.38 distinct answers, matches its base model's pass@32 at $T{=}0.7$ (0.704 against 0.695), gains 5.6 points from voting, and agreement is again the better error signal (0.794 against 0.602), at a cost of 4.2 points of pass@1; at $T{=}1.6$, 31\% of its answers are invalid. Its stated list stays closer to the reference than the base model's (0.722 against 0.779). For some models, then, much of the sampled narrowing is sharper sampling that a higher temperature undoes; the stated-list result is not.

\subsection{A third family: Mistral-7B v0.3}\label{sec:mistral}
We repeat the comparison with Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, a family also studied by \citet{jang2026cannotsample}. With the zero-shot prompt the base model does not answer: 52\% of its samples at $T{=}0.7$ and 76\% at $T{=}1$ are invalid (it continues the document instead), and its valid answers mostly repeat the first listed condition, so its single-call accuracy is 6\% and the zero-shot pair is not informative. With the few-shot prompt of \S\ref{sec:fewshot} the base model answers validly (0.4\% invalid), and the pair reproduces the other families (Table~\ref{tab:third}). Post-training raises pass@1 by 14.0 points and lowers pass@32 by 14.5, cuts distinct answers from 5.0 to 1.3 and Cov@32 by 0.138, and moves the samples away from the reference (TV $+0.058$). Voting gains the base model 5.6 points and the Instruct model 0.8; the three most frequent of 32 samples beat one list by 12.6 points for the base model, while for the Instruct model the list is ahead by 7.0; agreement is the better error signal for the base model (AUROC 0.826 against 0.671) and the stated probability for the Instruct model (0.747 against 0.613). The stated list improves modestly (TV $-0.036$ and DDF1 $+0.046$, surviving clustering and Holm correction over 42 tests; $-0.023$ with the true pathology removed, which does not), the smallest gain of the three families, and 11 of the 17 severe conditions are named less often. Mistral-Instruct also names 0.4 conditions per list that are not among the 49.

\subsection{Thinking mode}\label{sec:thinking}
Qwen3-8B can reason in a thinking block before it answers; the main runs turn this off. With thinking on (zero-shot prompt, $T{=}0.7$, up to 8,192 tokens; traces average about 1,500 tokens and all end before the limit), the same weights behave differently (Table~\ref{tab:third}). Accuracy rises at both ends: pass@1 is 0.671 against 0.559 without thinking, and pass@32 is 0.815, above both the non-thinking model (0.607) and the base model (0.732; $+8.4$ $[1.7,15.3]$ points, not surviving Holm correction over 42 tests). The samples are still narrow (1.76 distinct answers against 3.77 for the base model; Cov@32 0.283 against 0.323) but no farther from the reference than the base model's (TV 0.745 against 0.750). The stated list is the closest of any 7--8B model (TV 0.670, against 0.700 without thinking). The shift in error signals reverses: agreement is again the better signal (AUROC 0.693 with five samples, against 0.638 for the stated probability), as for base models, and voting gains 1.5 points (n.s.). The narrowing of the samples and the advantage of the stated probability are therefore properties of answering directly, which a reasoning mode largely undoes, consistent with the chain-of-thought prompt of \S\ref{sec:robust}.

\subsection{Averaging stated lists across calls}\label{sec:aggregate}
Stated lists can be averaged across calls, as samples can be voted. For each case we average the first 5 or 32 valid stated lists, each renormalized over the conditions it names, and score the mean against the reference (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}). Averaging helps base models far more than post-trained ones, whose lists vary little between calls (with the zero-shot prompt, 32 averaged lists name 7.6--13.4 conditions, against 16.6--28.8 for base models). The mean of five base-model lists is about as close to the reference as one post-trained list (Qwen3-8B 0.699 against 0.700, Qwen2.5-7B 0.717 against 0.717, OLMo~3.1 32B 0.691 against 0.701). With five lists on both sides, the post-trained mean is closer for Qwen2.5-7B under both prompts ($-0.025$ and $-0.027$) and for Qwen2.5-32B and Qwen3-8B under one prompt each, not significantly closer for OLMo~3.1 32B (zero-shot) and Mistral (few-shot), and farther for OLMo~3 7B and few-shot OLMo~3.1 32B. Averaged base lists also name severe conditions at least as often, and more often for OLMo~3.1 32B, OLMo~3 7B, Mistral and few-shot Qwen2.5-32B (by 0.06--0.14). Direct-answer post-training thus mostly packs into one call the differential that the base model spreads across calls, as \citet{lake2025overton} find for opinions, rather than adding information that averaged base lists lack. Qwen3-8B's thinking mode goes furthest: five of its lists beat five base-model lists by more than any direct-answer model's (TV $-0.049$) and name more severe conditions ($+0.129$). For a post-trained model answering directly, averaging five lists instead of reading one improves TV by only 0.005--0.025 at $T{=}0.7$ (up to 0.038 at $T{=}1$); it names 6--17 points more severe conditions, mostly by naming one to three more conditions. Five post-trained lists still cover 0.44--0.50 of the reference mass at $T{=}0.7$ (OLMo~3 7B 0.37) with six to eight conditions, about twice what their 32 samples cover (0.22--0.28) and as much as a uniform guesser naming about 24 conditions (0.48), so the coverage deficit of \S\ref{sec:sampled} is one of the sampled readout.

\subsection{Which signal to use: combination and held-out audit}\label{sec:audit}
Whether the stated probability or agreement across samples is the better error signal varies across models, so we ask what predicts it and whether a practitioner can tell. We pool 56 model--setting combinations scored on DDXPlus: the base/post-trained pairs at both temperatures and with three prompts, the OLMo stages, the 32B models, the medical models, our GRPO runs, and the full-distribution prompt. They share weights, prompts and temperatures, so we treat the correlations below as descriptive.
The stated probability's advantage over five-sample agreement correlates with the \emph{single-answer rate}, the share of cases whose five samples all give the same top answer (Spearman 0.75), with the stated probability's own AUROC (0.82; the gap contains this term, so part of this is by construction), and negatively with the number of distinct answers ($-0.74$). The stated probability is the better signal for 19 of the 56. No single-answer threshold separates them: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct still favors agreement, while OLMo~3 7B's DPO and Instruct models favor their unusually informative stated probability.
Combining the signals beats choosing between them. Fitting a logistic model of correctness on the stated probability and five-sample agreement with 100 labeled cases, and evaluating on the other 880, gives a mean held-out AUROC of 0.753 for the 39 post-trained combinations (0.765 for all 56); averaging the two signals' ranks without fitting gives 0.746; picking the better single signal on the same 100 cases gives 0.720; always using agreement 0.695; always using the stated probability 0.671. The logistic combination beats the single-signal pick for 35 of the 39 post-trained combinations. This matches the general finding that hybrids of consistency and verbalized confidence beat either~\citep{xiong2024express}.
For choosing a single signal, the simplest rule, always using agreement, is right for 66\% of the combinations (54\% of the 39 post-trained ones) and loses 0.025 AUROC on average (0.032). An audit does better: choose the signal with the higher AUROC on 100 random labeled cases, using five samples per case, and evaluate the choice on the other 880. Over 500 draws per combination it picks the better held-out signal 86\% of the time (82\% for post-trained models) and loses 0.005 AUROC (0.007); with 50 cases, 82\% and 0.010; with 200, 90\% and 0.003. It is reliable when the two signals differ by more than 0.05 AUROC (96\% of draws, 39 combinations) and close to a coin flip when they differ by less than 0.02 (53\%, 5 combinations), where the choice matters least. The audit's main cost is gold labels for 100 cases from the target population, and we validate it only within DDXPlus.


\section{Medical post-training and rare disease}\label{app:medrare}
\subsection{Medical post-training}\label{sec:medical}
Applied after instruction tuning, medical RL does not cost coverage. AlphaMed improves on its parent at every $k$ (Figure~\ref{fig:alphamed}): +3.9 points at $k{=}1$ ($[+2.2,+5.5]$; clustered $[-0.4,+8.4]$), widening to +8.4 at $k{=}32$ ($[+6.0,+10.6]$; clustered $[+2.6,+14.7]$). It reaches at least one correct sample for 47 of 49 conditions, against 39 for its parent.
It does not restore breadth, though: distinct answers (1.87 vs.\ 1.82), entropy (0.28 vs.\ 0.30), and coverage (0.27 vs.\ 0.24) stay at the instruction-tuned level, far below the base model.
AlphaMed also names off-list conditions more often than its parent, a mild loss of instruction following.
By condition, AlphaMed slightly \emph{raises} consideration in every severity tier (+2.2 to +4.3 points; Table~\ref{tab:severity}). It still loses acute pulmonary edema (0.31 to 0.13), and it introduces an attractor of its own: spontaneous pneumothorax as the top answer in 10.3\% of samples, against 4.3\% for its parent and a reference rate of 1.0\%.
Medical post-training thus operates on an already collapsed distribution; the collapse comes from the general post-training stage.
The other two descendants go further (Table~\ref{tab:medical}). HuatuoGPT-o1 (SFT then PPO) and m1 (SFT distillation from a reasoning teacher) both \emph{widen} the differential relative to their parent: 2.49 and 3.29 distinct answers vs.\ 1.82, and coverage@32 of 0.31 and 0.33 vs.\ 0.24. Their pass@32 rises by 13.7 $[+11.3,+16.0]$ and 18.1 $[+15.5,+20.7]$ points (clustered $[8.0,20.0]$ and $[10.5,25.9]$). m1 gains almost nothing at $k{=}1$ (+0.9, interval includes zero), so its improvement lies entirely in breadth. Both reasoning-trained models are also less calibrated (ECE 0.17 and 0.21 vs.\ 0.11). Medical post-training on an instruction-tuned model thus never adds to the collapse, and reasoning distillation partially reverses it.

\paragraph{Medical RL from the base model.}
Alongside the instruct-initialized AlphaMed-7B, the AlphaMed authors released a companion checkpoint, AlphaMed-7B-base-rl, initialized from Qwen2.5-7B rather than Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (according to its configuration file). The paper~\citep{liu2025alphamed} describes only the instruct-initialized models: GRPO with a binary correctness reward on about 19k multiple-choice questions from MedQA and MedMCQA, with a 64-question by 8-sample batch for 300 steps. It does not document the base-initialized checkpoint, so we cannot rule out differences in data or hyperparameters, and we treat it as corroborating rather than controlled evidence.
It offers a third-party check on whether RL itself causes the collapse (Table~\ref{tab:medical}, lower block).
It does not. RL from the base model improves on the base at every $k$ ($+14.3$ $[+13.1,+15.7]$ points at $k{=}1$, $+9.5$ $[+7.4,+11.6]$ at $k{=}32$). It keeps 3.14 distinct answers, against 1.87 for the instruct-initialized AlphaMed.
Against that instruct-model AlphaMed, it is 2.2 $[0.4,3.9]$ points worse at $k{=}1$ and 10.8 $[+8.6,+13.2]$ points better at $k{=}32$.
Its severe-tier Considered@32 shows no detectable change relative to the base ($-0.7$ $[-3.0,+1.7]$ points), against $-12.4$ for instruction tuning and $-8.1$ for instruction tuning followed by AlphaMed's RL (Table~\ref{tab:severity}; all well below the chance levels of \S\ref{sec:collapse}).

\subsection{Rare disease: overconfidence without accuracy}\label{sec:rare}
On 1,401 Phenopacket Store cases covering 749 diseases, every 7--8B model is near the floor: $\passk{1}$ is 1.4--2.2\% and $\passk{32}$ 3.7--7.0\% for the Qwen-family models, and lower for OLMo~3 7B (2.5--4.5\%).
Stated confidence is not near the floor. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct states a mean top-1 probability of 0.51, and its RL-trained medical descendants state 0.67 (HuatuoGPT-o1) and 0.69 (AlphaMed), while their accuracy is about 2\%. ECE accordingly rises from 0.49 to 0.64--0.67; at this accuracy ECE mostly restates mean confidence, so we read it as a rise in confidence without a matching rise in accuracy.
The RL-trained medical models (AlphaMed; HuatuoGPT-o1 after SFT) thus state much higher confidence where they are least accurate. The effect also appears without instruction tuning: AlphaMed's base-initialized checkpoint is the most overconfident Qwen-family model (mean confidence 0.71, ECE 0.70, accuracy 1.7\%).
The medical descendants do gain a little accuracy: AlphaMed beats its parent at every $k$ ($+0.6$ $[+0.2,+0.9]$ points at $k{=}1$, $+1.0$ $[+0.1,+2.1]$ at $k{=}32$), HuatuoGPT-o1 at $k \le 8$, and m1 at $k \ge 2$ ($+1.4$ $[+0.4,+2.5]$ at $k{=}32$). These gains are an order of magnitude smaller than the rise in confidence.
m1, trained only with supervised reasoning traces (at its intended 8k-token budget), is the exception on calibration and diversity: it is the most diverse model (15.3 distinct answers per 32 samples, against 11.5 for the parent and 9.2 for HuatuoGPT-o1), and its confidence rises least (0.57).

General post-training reproduces the DDXPlus pattern on Qwen3. Qwen3-8B beats its base at $\passk{1}$ ($+0.7$ $[+0.4,+1.1]$) and trails it from $k{=}8$ on, by $-2.2$ $[-3.1,-1.3]$ points at $\passk{32}$ (a relative loss of 37\%), with half the distinct answers (6.4 vs.\ 13.4). For Qwen2.5 no pass@$k$ difference is significant.
Unlike medical RL, general post-training \emph{lowers} stated confidence here (0.61 to 0.46 for Qwen3, 0.61 to 0.51 for Qwen2.5), although base models also give invalid answers more often (8.5\% of Qwen3-8B-Base samples).
OLMo~3 does not share this: every stage of its pipeline states a mean confidence of 0.73--0.84 at about 1\% accuracy ($\passk{32}$ 2.5--4.5\%), and its SFT checkpoint is the most overconfident model we evaluate (confidence 0.84, ECE 0.83).
On the 500-case subset, our 8B GRPO runs (\S\ref{sec:training}) are indistinguishable from their base (all pass@$k$ differences within $\pm0.6$ points).

Accuracy depends strongly on when the source case report was published (Table~\ref{tab:era}). For every model, $\passk{32}$ is 5--10\% on cases published up to 2022 ($n{=}901$), at most 1.7\% on 2023--24 cases ($n{=}348$), and 2.6--4.6\% on 2025+ cases ($n{=}152$). The pattern is not monotone, since 2025+ cases score above 2023--24 cases, so case mix also matters; the gap between older and recent cases is consistent with, but does not establish, memorization of older case reports.

\section{GRPO with a group-level reward}\label{sec:training}
We also fine-tuned Qwen3-1.7B (instruct) and Qwen3-8B-Base with GRPO (LoRA, 200 steps, 16 samples per prompt, KL coefficient 0.04) on DDXPlus training patients disjoint from the test split, comparing the standard correctness reward (\texttt{acc}) with a group-level reward (\texttt{gm}). If $k_a$ of the $n$ samples in a GRPO group give top answer $a$, \texttt{gm} adds $\operatorname{clip}(n\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(a) - (k_a - 1), 0, 1)$, which pays an answer only while its condition is under-represented relative to its reference mass. At 1.7B, a boosted variant (weight 3, training temperature 1.2, so the two changes are confounded) raised pass@1 by 1.6 and distinct answers by 0.10 over \texttt{acc} (two seeds; intervals ignore seed variation). From Qwen3-8B-Base, both recipes reached nearly the official Qwen3-8B's top-1 accuracy (0.537 and 0.539 vs.\ 0.558) while keeping more than twice its distinct answers (2.7--2.9 vs.\ 1.27), and \texttt{gm} narrowed the base model less than \texttt{acc} (distinct answers $+0.14$ at equal pass@1). Neither changed Phenopacket accuracy. Because \texttt{gm} needs a reference differential at training time, which real clinical data rarely provide, and the runs train on the evaluation distribution, these are existence results only. Closely related rewards computed from group answer counts have appeared since: a witness advantage that trains models to sample the distributions they state~\citep{arabi2026samplewhat}, and a batch bonus that penalizes repeated answers~\citep{song2025outcome}; we do not compare against them. The GRPO runs enter only the 56-combination audit (\S\ref{sec:audit}).
